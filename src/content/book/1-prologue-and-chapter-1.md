## Prologue: The Last Stop

Somewhere over the Atlantic, at an hour that belonged to no clock in particular, a young American called JP was asking a machine whether it was rude to bring wine to dinner.

It was the small hours of Friday the twenty-third of October, 2026, which is to say more or less now. The overnight flight from New York was dark. JP had tried to sleep, first against the window and then against nothing at all, and both had failed. So he had taken his laptop out of the pack at his feet and opened it, and the screen had lit up and said *Welcome, JP*, the way it always did. He laid his finger on the key in the corner, and it let him in, because it knew his finger and nobody else's.

The plane's Wi-Fi had given up, and it didn't matter. The thing he was talking to didn't live on the internet. It lived inside the laptop, all of it, and it would answer almost anything you typed. Putting machines like that into offices that weren't allowed the internet was JP's job. Tonight he wasn't working.

He had already asked it to make an email to his manager sound less annoyed. He had written:

*Like I said three times before I left, it's ALL in the shared folder.*

And it had given him back:

> Hi Dana,
>
> Just a friendly reminder that everything you need is in the shared folder. Happy to walk you through it when I'm back!
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]

He had pasted in his packing list, and it had told him the list looked great, and October in Northern Ireland could be wet, so had he thought about a rain jacket? He was wearing the rain jacket.

Now he typed *is it rude to bring wine to dinner in Northern Ireland* and pressed Enter.

> Not rude at all! 🍷 Wine is a thoughtful, common gift for hosts in Northern Ireland. If they have kids, a small treat for them is a lovely touch too.

JP hadn't thought of the kids. There would be a shop in Ballyclare. He typed one more.

*best pub near Ballyclare that does food*

> Great question! Since I'm offline, I can't check current listings. A couple of tips:
>
> - **Check a map app** for recent reviews once you're connected.
> - **Ask your hosts!** Locals always know the best spots.

"Fair enough," said JP, to nobody, and shut the lid.

He didn't switch it off. He never did. With the lid shut it simply went to sleep, like a fire banked for the night, using a little of itself every hour to stay ready. He slid it into his pack and closed his eyes.

The plane came down into Dublin on a grey morning. JP's body came down with it, stiff in the knees and gritty in the eyes, but the rest of him was still somewhere over the sea.

Dublin's airport has no railway, so he took a bus to Connolly station. His friends had said to come for dinner, which gave him a whole day to go a hundred miles. He spent it the way you spend a day when you are too tired for anything else: slowly, and mostly sitting down.

The Enterprise took him to Belfast in a little over two hours. On the last stretch the lights in the carriage flickered, off and on again, so fast that he wasn't sure he had seen it. Nobody else looked up.

Belfast Grand Central was so new it still looked surprised to be there, all glass and light. His phone buzzed as he stepped off the train.

*Dinner's at 7, the kids are dying to see you.*

He smiled, and then he saw the corner of the screen: *23%*. He had meant to charge it on the train.

He did have a solar panel. It was strapped flat to the back of his pack, four black squares set in black cloth and folded like a slim book, with metal rings at the corners and a short cable tied to one edge. He had brought it for the coast walks his friends had promised, and hadn't unfolded it once. But you don't spread out a solar panel in a railway station, not unless you want to be looked at.

No bus to Ballyclare left from Grand Central, which seemed a strange thing for anywhere called Grand Central, so he walked.

He walked across Belfast as the afternoon went cold. He passed the City Hall, white stone under a green dome. Long before it was built, a great hall for selling linen stood on that very spot. JP didn't know that.

At Laganside, down by the river, he found the 153. He tapped his card, zipped his wallet into his jacket pocket and sat at the back. The bus went out into the country, and the light went low and yellow across the fields.

I don't know what JP expected Ballyclare to be like. I know he expected dinner.

When the bus came into the town he went up to the front.

"Is this downtown?"

"This is the Square," said the driver. "Mind yourself, now."

His friends had told him to stay on one more stop, but he had been sitting down since New York, so he got off a stop early. The bus sighed off up the road, and it was just JP and the Square. It was dry and cold and very still. There was an old hall in the middle, with a clock tower, and cars parked round it, and the shop windows were already lit. He took a photo, and then another, for no reason except that he had arrived.

He was well over an hour early for dinner. The map on his phone showed a river path a few minutes away, beside the Six Mile Water. He dropped the phone into the top pocket of his pack, pushed his hands into his pockets, and went down to it.

It is a curious thing, but the last hour of a long journey is always the longest. JP dawdled. The river talked to itself beside him. The sky went the colour of a cold peach, and then the sun was down, and up on the road the streetlights came on one by one, and windows lit in the houses above the river. Rooks went over very high, going home. Somewhere behind him was the steady hum of traffic. JP thought about the wine he would buy on the way back, and whether the kids would remember him, and whether it would be rude to fall asleep at dinner.

Then the lights went out.

They went out all at once, the streetlights and the windows and the whole town behind him, as if someone had pulled a single string. The noise of the traffic stopped. It didn't fade. It stopped, like a door shut on it.

JP stood still in the dark. He could smell smoke: not a bonfire, but coal smoke, thick and sour, the smell of a great many chimneys.

Then the ground under his feet tipped, the way a deck tips, and he went down into the dark.

## Chapter 1: The Man on the Riverbank

It had rained all night on Ballyclare, hard enough to wake Rebecca Boyd twice in the loft. Then, a little before morning, it stopped, as if it had just remembered it was the Sabbath.

It was Sunday the twenty-third of October. If that date sounds familiar, it should. But this was the year 1881, and nobody in Ballyclare had ever heard of a laptop, or of a young man called JP.

By the time the Boyds set out for meeting, the sky had been washed clean and blue, and the whole world dripped. Every chimney on Mill Row was smoking. It was coal smoke, thick and sour. Rebecca didn't notice it. Nobody does, when it is the smell of home.

Ma went first, because Ma always went first. She walked the way she sewed, quick and neat with nothing wasted, and her Sunday bonnet pointed at the meeting-house like a needle at the end of its thread.

Da came behind her in his Sunday blacks. All week he was the engineman at the paper mill and smelt of oil and steam. On Sundays he smelt of soap, and walked carefully, as if the blacks might crack.

Then came David, who was eleven, and never walked anywhere he could dawdle. Last came Rebecca, who was thirteen. She had left school a year ago to sew with Ma, and she minded that. She also minded David, which was harder.

The path ran below the backs of the houses, beside the Six Mile Water. The river was high and brown after the rain, and it talked to itself as it went. David found a stick, and then a better stick, and was just going to see how far it would float when Rebecca took it off him.

"It's the Sabbath," she said.

"I wasn't playing. I was only seeing."

"Seeing is playing, on the Sabbath."

"Why?"

"Because Ma says. And Ma has eyes in the back of her bonnet."

Ma didn't turn round. "Ma has," she said.

David walked properly for nearly a minute. Then he stopped so suddenly that Rebecca walked into him.

"Rebecca," he said. "There's a man."

There was.

He lay under the old hawthorn where the bank went down to the water. He was on his side, with his knees drawn up a little, the way you lie when you mean to rest for a minute and sleep for an hour. The thorn was red with haws and hung all over with raindrops, and the grass round him was soaked. He was dry.

Rebecca noticed that, and didn't know what to make of it, so she kept it, the way you keep a button you find in the road.

He was a young man, with a day's beard on him and his hair cropped close at the sides. His jacket hung open, and it shone like wet slate, though it wasn't wet at all.

On his back was a bag, held by two straps over his shoulders like a soldier's knapsack, but smooth, and not leather and not canvas. Strapped flat to the outside of it was a square of black stuff, folded like a book, with metal eyelets at the corners and a short black cord tied along one edge. Oilcloth, Rebecca thought. For sitting on, if you were the kind of man who sat on riverbanks.

"Look at his feet," whispered David. "His shoes are like a feed bag with laces."

They were. They were made of some woven stuff full of tiny holes, with laces like anybody's, and soles as thick as a farl and as white as a scrubbed table. Rebecca would have liked to draw them. It was the Sabbath, so she drew them in her head instead.

Quick feet came along the path behind them. It was Mrs Strahan from the top of the row, in her good black, carrying her Bible. Mrs Strahan was not a bad woman. She was a woman who had never once in her life been late for meeting, and did not mean to begin.

She looked at the man under the thorn. She looked at the Boyds. She didn't stop.

"We'll be late," she said, going by, "and him likely drunk."

Then she was round the bend of the river and gone.

"She'll have told the whole meeting," said Da, "before the precentor has the first psalm started."

Ma was already going down the bank. She bent over the man and said, "Sir," the way you speak to a gentleman. When that did nothing, she said, "Here, you, wake up," the way you speak to a tramp. That did nothing either. She shook his shoulder, and Da shook it harder, and the man's eyes stayed shut.

Ma bent closer and sniffed at his breath, the way she sniffed the milk on a warm morning.

"No drink," she said. "Not a drop."

Da laid his big hand flat on the man's chest and waited. "He's breathing steady," he said. "Like a boiler fire banked for the night." Then he looked along the path after Mrs Strahan, and said, not crossly, but the way he'd say a valve was sticking, "We'll miss the meeting."

"It's a work of mercy," said Ma.

Da nodded, as if that settled it, which it did. "Then we'll want a door."

Sam Orr from the mill came running along the path just then, late as usual, with his collar undone.

"Sam," said Da. "You'll not be needed at meeting this morning."

Sam's face lit up. "Will I not, Mr Boyd?"

So Da and Sam went back up to the row and lifted the Boyds' own back door off its hinges. Down at the river, Da slid the straps of the bag off the man's arms, gently, as you'd take the pack off a sleeping pedlar. Then they lifted the man onto the door. He didn't wake, not even when Sam dropped his end an inch.

David picked up the bag and nearly sat down. "It's heavy!"

"Then carry it between you," said Ma. "And don't you go hoking in it."

That was how the Boyds came home from meeting before they had ever got there: Da and Sam with the door, and the man on it, and Ma alongside with a hand on his shoulder in case he rolled, and David and Rebecca behind with the bag swinging between them and the black cloth bumping hard at Rebecca's knees.

The Boyds' pew stood empty that morning, and it was talked about.

It is not easy to get a man on a door through a door, but they managed it. Then Da and Sam stood in the kitchen with the door between them, and waited to be told where to put him.

Ma looked at the man. Rebecca could see her thinking, because Ma thought with her mouth, pressing it smaller and smaller.

Rebecca knew what she was weighing. There was the settle bed by the fire, which was where you put a tramp, or a cousin, or a man off the road. There was the loft, but that was the children's, and you couldn't get a door up a ladder. And there was the room, with Ma and Da's own bed in it and the good quilt on top, which was where you would put the minister, if the minister ever needed putting anywhere.

The trouble was that nobody could tell what the man was.

His jacket said gentleman. It was finer stuff than anything in Ballyclare, and it whispered when you touched it, like a lady's silk. His shoes said tramp. His hands were soft and clean, with no work in them at all, which said gentleman again, or else a clerk, which was worse. And his hair had been cropped at the sides like a man just out of gaol and left long on top like a dandy's, so it said both at once and was no help to anybody.

"He's not getting any lighter," said Da.

"The room," said Ma. "Our bed."

She looked round at them all, in case anybody had an opinion. Nobody had.

"If he's a gentleman, he'll expect it," said Ma. "And if he's not, he'll need it more."

Ma put the children out in the yard while she and Da got him into the bed, which took a long while and a great deal of Da saying "Easy, now." Sam hung the door again and went off up the row to tell everybody.

When Rebecca was let back in, the man lay under the good quilt with only his jacket and shoes off. The shoes stood side by side on the hearth like two loaves set to rise. When David pressed the sole of one with his thumb, it gave like dough, and then it pushed back.

The jacket was on the kitchen table, and Ma sat in front of it the way she sat in front of a hard piece of sewing.

"He has people," said Ma. "Somebody's watching the road for him this minute. We'll find his name, and we'll write."

She patted the jacket all over. There was something flat and hard in one of the pockets at the front. But when Ma went to put her hand in, there was no way in. The pocket had no button and no flap. It had no mouth at all.

Down each side of the front of the jacket ran a strip of tiny teeth, like the edge of a comb. Rebecca had taken them for trimming. Now she saw that the same teeth ran across the pocket too, only here they were shut together, tight.

"He's had his money sewn in," said Ma, "like a drover going to the fair."

Rebecca leaned close. She knew sewing the way Da knew engines, and this was not sewing. There wasn't a stitch in it. The two rows of teeth were simply holding on to each other, like the fingers of two hands clasped.

"There's a wee tab," said David. "At the end. Look."

There was: a little flat tab, of some stuff that wasn't metal and wasn't horn, hanging from the end of the teeth like a bell-pull. David gave it a good tug, like a gentleman ringing for his tea.

"You'll break it," said Rebecca.

"I won't." He waggled it, and it rode on the teeth like a bead on a wire. He pushed it towards the end, and it wouldn't go, so he pulled it the other way.

The teeth came apart. They came apart behind the tab, one after another, all along the pocket, the way a seam opens when you pull the thread, only faster and with a small purring noise. And there was the mouth of the pocket, open.

Nobody said anything at all.

David pushed the tab back. The teeth bit shut behind it, every one, and the pocket was closed as if it had never been open.

In a voice of deep happiness, David said, "Teeth. Teeth that bite closed."

He did it again, and again. Ma let him do it three times. Then she took his hand away and did it herself, slowly, with her lips pressed together, the way she threaded a needle by bad light. The teeth came apart for her too.

"Well," said Ma, and that was all she said.

You and I would call it a zip, and never look at one twice. Nobody in Ballyclare had ever seen one. Nobody in Ireland had.

Inside the pocket was a pocket-book of good black leather, folded in two. Ma opened it, and her mouth went small. There was paper money in it, green, with THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA printed along the top. Ma didn't count it. She shut that side of the pocket-book at once, the way you'd shut a door on somebody dressing.

"He's a Yank!" said David.

In the other side were cards, stiff and thin as horn, with the corners rounded off, each slid into its own wee pocket. One had his likeness on it.

Ma held it at arm's length, and then nearer, and then further away again. Then she gave it to Rebecca, because Ma's eyes were not what they had been.

It was him, grey and silvery, and finer than any photograph Rebecca had ever seen. The only photograph in the Boyds' house was the likeness Ma's brother had sent from America, and that was brown, and stiff, and made him look as if he had swallowed a poker. This was like looking through a very small window at the man himself, on a grey day.

"It's an engraving," said Ma, over her shoulder. "Like the Queen's head on a stamp."

Whoever had engraved it, Rebecca thought, must have used a needle, and the patience of a saint.

The card round it was printed in fine blue and gold lines, as close as the threads in muslin. Across the top it said NEW YORK STATE. The rest was crowded with small print and figures, like a railway bill: a name in capitals that ended JOSEPH PATRICK, a number and a street, and at one side, in a wee clear pane, his face again, smaller, with the same name curving round it.

"Joseph Patrick," Rebecca read out.

"That'll be the engraver," said Ma, "and his shop. Your uncle's has the photographer's name on it, the same way."

"It might be his," said Rebecca.

"And who puts his own name round his own face?" said Ma. "The Queen does, on a penny. Nobody else."

"And there's no writing to a picture-man in New York," said Da, from the room door. "He'll have taken half America."

Then Rebecca found a date. Most of it she could make nothing of, but the end of it was plain enough.

"Nineteen ninety-seven," she said.

David laughed out loud. Ma took the card back and held it at arm's length and looked for herself.

"A printer's error," said Ma.

"It's a long way out, for an error," said Rebecca.

"Then it was a very bad printer," said Ma. She put the card back, and the pocket-book in the pocket, and ran the wee tab along, and the pocket bit itself shut. Then she hung the jacket on the back of the room door, and that was the end of looking for his name.

It was his driving licence, and 1997 was the year he was born. But there was nobody in Ballyclare to tell the Boyds so, and every reason in the world to blame the printer.

Da went for Dr Hamilton as soon as meeting was out.

Dr Hamilton came in the middle of the afternoon, in his tall hat, looking as tired as he always looked. He sat on the edge of the bed and took the man's wrist, and looked at his watch for a long time. He lifted one of the man's eyelids with his thumb, and then the other, and felt all over his head.

"Has he a name?"

"Not that we can find," said Ma. "He's a Yank."

"Is he, now." The doctor's eye went through the open door to the shoes on the hearth, and stayed there a while. He didn't say a word about them. Rebecca thought that was very grand of him.

"He's insensible," he said at last. "The pulse is good. Better than mine. There's no fever, and no blow to the head, and no drink. It's a trance, like thon girl in England."

"What girl?" said David, and was hushed.

"Milk," said Dr Hamilton. "By the spoon. A wee drop at a time, and wait till he swallows. If he swallows, he'll do."

Ma fetched a cup and tried a spoonful there and then. Everybody watched the man's throat. It moved. He swallowed.

"There," said Dr Hamilton, and reached for his hat.

"And the girl," said Ma. "Did she wake?"

"She did," said the doctor.

It wasn't until he had gone that Rebecca noticed he hadn't said when.

All that afternoon the man's bag sat on the floor beside the bed, with the black cloth strapped to its back, and all that afternoon David looked at it. He looked at it from the doorway, and from the foot of the bed, and from flat on the floor with his chin on his hands.

"It has teeth too," he said. "All round the top. There might be a letter in it, with his name on. For finding his people."

"It isn't ours," said Rebecca.

"I'd only look."

"It isn't ours," said Rebecca again. She knelt and pushed it under the bed, all the way to the wall, black cloth and all, where nobody could get at it. She didn't open it. She didn't even want to, much.

Late in the afternoon Mrs McCready came in from next door with a bowl of broth under a cloth. She was old and small and bent like a hook, and she had taught Rebecca every stitch Rebecca knew. She set the broth on the hob. Then she went into the room and stood at the foot of the bed for a long time, looking at the man.

She didn't ask who he was, or where he had come from, or what Mrs Strahan was saying about him, which by then was a great deal.

"Somebody's son," she said at last, and went home.

Dusk came early, the way it does at the end of October. When the window began to go grey, Da went down to the mill to see to the boiler fires, as he did every Sabbath evening, because a boiler has to be minded whatever day it is. Ma made up the settle bed for herself and Da, and put her bonnet back on.

"I'm away up the row to Mrs Strahan's," she said, "to tell her he's not drunk." And she went, with the face she kept for burnt porridge.

That left Rebecca by the bed with the cup and the spoon, because it was her turn, and David on the floor, looking at the place where the bag had gone. The window went from grey to a darker grey. The man swallowed when Rebecca gave him the milk, and slept, and swallowed, and slept.

Then, from under the bed, inside the man's bag, something began to sing.
