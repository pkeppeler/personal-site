"I don't know," said Rebecca.

She said it straight out, as she would have said it to the Master. It was the second time since yesterday, and both times about the number in the corner. Rebecca generally knew. When she didn't, she said, "That depends."

"In the interest sums, it's eight pounds in every hundred," she said. "But there you know it's pounds. You can't do a sum when you don't know what you've a hundred of."

"It's more than seven, anyway," said David.

"Hush."

They knelt side by side on the boards and looked at it. The writing machine lay open on the floor by the bed, and its glass had gone the dull grey of a window at dusk. The box sat in its corner with the two lines, *Battery low* and *8% remaining*, and the wee coffin in the box had hardly any red left: a sliver at one end.

David knew that red. It was the last of a fire when everybody has gone to bed and nobody has put on coal: one small glow at the bottom of the grate, and all the rest gone grey round it. On Monday he had decided the glass was only banked, like a boiler fire. But a banked fire keeps. This one looked to him as if nobody had banked it right, and it was going out.

He put his hand flat on the metal beside the letters, without quite meaning to.

It was cold. On Sunday, coming out of the bag, it had been smooth and cool. Now it was cold right through, like the latch of the back door on a frosty morning.

"Feel it," he whispered.

Rebecca touched the backs of her fingers to it, as Ma touched hers to a forehead, and took them away again.

"It's starved," she said.

"It's going out," said David. "Look at the red."

Rebecca was looking at the coffin, and the word *low*, and at her own fingers, and David could see her adding it all up.

"When anybody's low and cold with it," she said slowly, "you keep them warm. When Mrs McCready took her bad turn last winter, Ma put her to the fire in the grey shawl and sat up with her all night. She came round by morning."

"And when our fire goes out," said David, "Ma sends me in next door for a live coal. You put it in among the dead ones, and they take."

They had come to the same place by two roads, and they both looked at the room door. Beyond it, in front of the kitchen fire, Ma and Da were asleep in the settle bed, and Da was snoring.

"Not tonight," said Rebecca.

"Tomorrow, then. When Ma's at Mrs McCready's."

Every Saturday after dinner, Ma went in next door to wash Mrs McCready's hair for the Sabbath, because Mrs McCready's arms wouldn't go up that far any more. She was never back under the hour. There was always the talk.

Rebecca shut the lid on the eight, gently, and they went up the ladder to bed.

On Saturday it rained. David carried in the water and the coal for the Sabbath, and Rebecca sat at the frame with Ma and sewed as if there were nothing under the bed in the room but a bag. The mill hands stopped at two on a Saturday, but an engine doesn't put itself to bed, and Da was never home till he had seen to it.

After dinner Ma put her own shawl over her head against the rain, took the soap and a towel, and went in next door.

The old grey shawl stayed on its peg behind the door. That was the one for anybody poorly.

David had the writing machine out from under the bed before the latch had dropped. He set it on the quilt, one hand flat on the bottom, and lifted the lid, and the lake came up dim among its mountains. Rebecca fetched the man's right hand from the door side and laid the first finger on the little circle. The lake slid away, and there was the page, with *Welcome, JP* across the top.

The box was in the corner again, as if it had sat up all night waiting for them. The number said *6*. It had lain shut and untouched since last night.

"So much for resting," said David.

He carried it through to the kitchen, open and lit, holding it level in both hands, as if it were brimful and might spill. Rebecca took the grey shawl down from its peg. Between them they wrapped the writing machine in it, lid and all, until the glass looked out of the wool, pale, like a sick child's face out of a blanket. Rebecca turned one corner back, so they could see the number.

David drew the low stool in close to the fire and set the bundle on it, facing the room, with its back to the coals.

"Not so near," said Rebecca.

"It has to take," said David. But he moved it back an inch, to be fair.

They sat on the floor and watched the corner of the glass. The kettle on the crook began to tick. Rain ran down the window behind the frame. The number said *6*, and went on saying it.

Then the writing machine made a sound.

It was so faint at first that David took it for the kettle. But the kettle was behind him, and this was in front: a thin, steady whirr, like a bee shut in a jar, and it was coming out of the writing machine. With it came a breath of warm air, out of a row of fine slits along its back, just under the hinge.

In a whole week, it was the first sound of its own they had ever heard it make.

David's heart jumped. He knew a machine getting going when he heard one. When the big engine at the mill began to turn of a Monday morning, the whole engine house took up a hum, low and steady, that came up through the soles of your boots.

"It's taken!" he said. "It's going, like the engine!"

Rebecca had put her hand out to the slits. The warm breath came against her palm, and kept coming, and didn't stop.

"It's breathing," she said, and her voice changed. "David. It's breathing quick."

She put the backs of her fingers to the lid, above the slits, and snatched them away.

"That's the fire," said David. "That's what we wanted."

"Not that much, you don't." She had the shawl round her hands and the writing machine up off the stool before he was on his feet. "It's burning. It's took a fever."

David put one finger on the back of it, where the shawl had fallen away, and had it off again in a moment. The shawl, where it had hung nearest the coals, smelt of hot wool, and of something worse than hot wool.

Rebecca carried it to the window and set it on the deep sill where Ma kept the milk cool, with the rain-cold glass of the window at its back. She threw the back door open, and the cold air came in with the smell of the wet yard. A fever wants cooling. Her hand went out to the basin on the dresser, for a wet cloth.

It stopped halfway.

David saw her remember. *Nor the river, nor the rain-barrel, nor the jug on the washstand.* She had said it herself, on Thursday. She took her hand back, and took up the corner of her apron instead, and fanned the writing machine with that.

David knelt beside her with nothing to do with his hands.

The whirr went on, thin and quick, while the cold came round them both. Then it slowed. Then it stopped, and the warm breath out of the slits went with it, and the writing machine lay on the sill as quiet as it had ever been.

Rebecca had sat up with a good many sick people in her thirteen years, and it was just as well. A box like that one will put up with a great deal of carrying, and shaking, and pressing in all the wrong places, but it is about as fond of a fire as a sugar mouse.

They looked at the corner of the glass.

The number said *5*.

While they watched, the box slid away down out of sight and came straight back up again: *Battery low*, and *5% remaining*, and in the wee coffin no more red than a pin's head.

"It went down," said David.

"It went down for the fire." Rebecca sat back on her heels.

"It went down for resting," said David, "and it went down for warming. It goes down whatever you do."

Behind them the kettle had come to the boil and was rattling its lid, and neither of them got up to it.

David looked at the writing machine on the sill, with the box in the corner of its glass and the letters waiting under it. It wrote whatever you started it on. It had written his sums, and his lines, and his composition. It had written Rebecca a lesson and a poem, and the letter that was to nobody. It had never once said no.

"Have it write a cure," he said.

Rebecca turned and looked at him.

"Start it on one," said David. "Like the sums. You put in the start, and it writes the rest. It wrote you a lesson. It can write a cure."

"It's low. You don't keep anybody low writing. I said."

"Ma wakes him for his milk," said David. "Every night. You'd wake anybody for their physic."

He saw her see it.

"One," said Rebecca. "And I'll write it."

She stood at the sill with her hands over the letters, and David could see her working out how a cure was set out. There were always a few in Da's paper, in big black letters: A CERTAIN CURE FOR, and then a list of everything it would mend, from headaches to lowness of spirit. First you put what it was for, and the cure came after.

She found the letters one at a time, and left out *certain*, since she wasn't.

*a cure for a low battery*

And she pressed Enter.

> Here's how to make your battery last longer! 🔋
>
> - **Lower the brightness.**
> - **Turn on Battery Saver.**
> - **Close any apps** you're not using.
> - **Hibernate** when you step away.
> - **Plug in** as soon as you can!

There was the green picture again, upright and full to the top. Rebecca said it was a jar. David still thought it was a coffin, and had the sense not to say so.

"*Make your battery last longer*," Rebecca read. "So it's a cure."

"It's a list," said David.

"Cures are lists."

They went down it together.

"*Battery Saver*," Rebecca read. "With capitals on it, like a name. It's a physic, like the ones in the paper." She frowned. "We'd have to send to Belfast for it."

"*Close any apps*," David read. "What's apps?"

Rebecca turned the word over and didn't care for it. "Whatever they are, it says close them, so they're open."

David looked round the kitchen for anything open. There was only the back door, with the rain blowing in past it. He got up and shut it, firmly, to be on the safe side.

"*Plug in*," Rebecca read. "We've had that one."

"I never said a word."

"You were going to."

That left two.

"*Lower the brightness*," said Rebecca slowly. "We put it in the brightest place in the house. You keep a sickroom dim. Ma pulls the blind down for anybody bad, and stands the candle behind the jug."

"*Lower*," said David. "So you put it down low, somewhere dark." He was pleased. It was the first part of the cure that made any sense. "And *hibernate*. What's that?"

"It's what hedgehogs do."

David stared at her.

"It was in a lesson in the school book," said Rebecca, who never lost a word once she had it. "When the frosts come, the hedgehog makes himself a nest of dead leaves under a hedge, and sleeps the whole winter through without a bite. He goes as cold as a stone, and you could pick him up and he'd never wake. That's hibernating. In the spring he gets up and goes about his business."

David had seen one once, in the leaves against the lade wall, rolled so tight you couldn't tell which end was which. Sam Orr said they got into the byres at night and milked the cows.

"*When you step away*," David read. "So it wants to go to sleep, like a hedgehog, in the dark. And be left."

"Somewhere cold," said Rebecca. She looked at the fire, and at the shawl on the floor. "And we had it roasting."

"Under the hedge, then," said David. "In the leaves, like a real one."

"In the rain?" said Rebecca. "There's the press in the room. It's dark, and it's cold, and nobody opens it but Ma."

If either of them had looked along the top row of keys, above the figures, they would have found two with a wee sun printed on each, one sun small and one large. The small one would have turned the glass down in a moment, like the wick of a lamp. But you don't go pressing a sun, if you have never seen it done, and neither of them looked.

Rebecca shut the lid, and the glass went out.

The cupboard stood in the corner of the room on the wall side, as far from the fire as the house allowed. Ma kept the good blankets in it, and the bottom shelf held nothing but a candle-box. They put the writing machine on that shelf on its own, with nothing over it, since it had had enough of shawls for one day, and David shut the door on it softly, in case it woke.

"Now we leave it," said Rebecca. "That's *step away*."

They both stepped back from the cupboard, as if it might mind.

Ma came in at the back door with rain on her shoulders, and stopped on the mat, and sniffed.

"What's burning?"

The grey shawl was back on its peg, still warm. "The shawl was too near the fire, Ma," said Rebecca.

Ma took it down. She found the place at once: a brown mark on the fold, the size of a penny, where the wool had gone crisp. She put her thumb on it. Then she looked at the stool, which had been put back crooked, and at the kettle, which had boiled itself near dry, and at the pair of them.

"Too near the fire," said Ma, "doing what?"

David looked at his boots. Rebecca looked at Ma and said nothing at all, which was not like her.

"Well," said Ma.

She folded the shawl with the mark inside, and laid it over her arm, and went to fill the kettle, and she didn't ask again. But all the rest of that afternoon David felt her look come across the kitchen now and then and rest on him, like a hand.

Da came home wet from the mill, and had his tea, and fell asleep in his chair with his boots to the fire. At bedtime Ma gave the man his milk herself.

"He'll do," she told him, as she told him every night now, since Dr Hamilton had said it first, and she tucked his hand in.

When she came back to the kitchen she stood a moment listening to the rain. "It's turning cold," she said. "That man wants the other blanket." And she turned for the room.

David was up before she had taken a step.

"I'll get it!"

He was in the room and at the cupboard before she could answer. He opened the door just wide enough for his arm, pulled the top blanket off the pile, and shut the door again with his heel. When he brought the blanket out, Ma took it from him and put the back of her hand to his forehead.

"You're not hot," she said.

"No, Ma."

"Then you're up to something."

"Leave the boy be," said Da, from his chair, with his eyes shut. "It'll not last."

It was David's turn for the last milk. Ma and Da were in the settle bed, and Da was snoring again, and David took the cup and the candle into the room. Rebecca came down the ladder after him in her stocking feet.

He gave the man his milk first, a drop at a time, because that came first.

Then they opened the cupboard and took the writing machine out of the dark. It was cool now, and only cool. David lifted the lid, and the lake came up dimmer than he had ever seen it. Rebecca fetched the man's hand, and the welcome came, and the page.

The box came with it. There was no red in the wee coffin now that he could see. The number in the corner said *2*.

"It's only gone down three," David whispered. "All afternoon and all evening, and only three."

"Out of five," said Rebecca.

David hadn't thought of it that way, and wished she hadn't either.

Two left. Two in the hundred, of whatever it was. If there were only two, then whatever it wrote next had better be the most important thing in the world. He sat with his finger over the letters and waited for the most important thing in the world to come to him.

It didn't.

He thought of sums, and was ashamed of himself. He thought of the Master, and of Belfast, and of the hundred lines, and every one of them was so small he could have put it in his pocket. He thought of a composition, set as the Master set them: *The Writing Machine, its parts and uses*. It would be the best composition ever handed in at the national school, and the Master would have to read it out. But that was for him, and you didn't spend the last two of anything on yourself.

"A letter," he said suddenly. "To his people. Saying he's here, and safe. That's the most important thing there is."

"It doesn't know them."

"You don't know it doesn't."

"I do," said Rebecca. "I tried. On Wednesday night. It wrote me *Dear*, and a blank, and *Your Name* at the bottom." She looked at the bed. "It doesn't know who he is."

So that was the letter, the one to nobody. David was sorry he had asked.

"David," whispered Rebecca.

"I'm thinking."

He was. Somewhere there was a thing to write, a great big one, that would make all the difference. He could nearly see it, like a sum he knew the answer to and couldn't get the working for, and while he waited the glass began to dim.

Rebecca was looking at the man. He lay as he always lay, with his face to the ceiling and his hands under the quilt, breathing slow and even.

"Have it write that he'll wake," she said.

She said it as Ma said *He'll do* over the cup, as if a thing said often enough had to come true. David looked at her, and she didn't look back.

It was the start of a thing, and the machine always wrote you the rest. It would write when, and how, and that he'd be well, and go home to his people. And once it was written down, there it would be, in black and white, like the sums, every one of them right.

He found the letters one at a time, so as not to waste any.

*jp will wake up*

He pressed Enter.

> I don't have information about JP's condition. If someone is unresponsive, please contact emergency services right away.

In the corner, the two had gone to *1*.

There was no *when*, and no *how*, and no *well*. It had written something else.

"*I don't have information*," Rebecca read, very low. "It doesn't know how he is, either." She came to the long word and took it a bit at a time. "*Un-re-spon-sive.* Not answering."

"That's him," said David.

"*Please contact emergency services right away.*"

"Contact's touching," said David. "We're forever touching him."

"An emergency's when the chimney goes on fire." Rebecca looked at it a while longer. "It means fetch help. Anybody, right away." She let her breath out. "As if we'd only this minute found him under the thorn, and never thought of the doctor."

The doctor. David looked at his four words on the glass, and thought he saw the trouble. It was the start. Copy a sum off the board wrong, and it came out wrong however well you worked it. He would give it a better start, with the doctor in it: *jp will wake up the doctor says he will do*. He had every word of it ready.

He put his finger out to the letters.

The glass went black.

It didn't dim first, as it did when it was left waiting, and it didn't go grey. It stopped, as the singing had stopped on the first night when he pressed *Stop*, and the quiet afterwards filled the room right up to the ceiling.

David pressed a letter. Nothing. He shut the lid and counted ten, as he had on Monday, and lifted it, and the glass stayed black: no lake, no mountains, nothing. Rebecca brought the man's hand round and laid the first finger on the little circle herself, and held it there, gently, a long while.

Nothing.

"It's at nought," said Rebecca at last. "It went to one, and now it's at nought."

So that was what happened at nought. On Thursday David had been sure it would go off, like an alarum, as the wee glass had sung in the bag. There was no bell. Nothing went off at all. It only went.

There was a word for it, and he didn't say it, not over the man's head.

Rebecca didn't put it back in the cupboard. She knelt and drew the man's bag out from under the bed, and slid the writing machine into its own padded pocket at the bottom, where they had found it, and ran the teeth shut.

"Hedgehogs wake up in the spring," said David.

Rebecca pushed the bag back under the bed, black cloth and all, where it had lain on the very first day. She didn't say anything. She tucked the man's hand under the quilt and went up the ladder.

It rained on the Sabbath, as it had rained all week, steady and grey and in no hurry.

Mrs McCready came in to sit with the man, so that all four Boyds could go to meeting. "Away on, the lot of you," she said, settling into the chair by the window. "A pew that's empty two Sabbaths running gets a name for itself."

They went the river way, and the Six Mile Water was high and brown.

At the meeting-house the heads turned when the Boyds came in, as Ma had known they would, and turned back again slowly. The precentor raised the psalm, and the meeting took it up after him, slow and strong, with no instrument but their own voices.

The minister rose for the long prayer, and the whole meeting stood, as it always did, and shut its eyes.

He prayed for the Queen, and for the harvest got in, and for those at sea. He named the sick of the congregation one by one: old Mr Crawford and his chest, the Gilmores' baby, Mrs Strahan's sister in Larne. And last of all, after a little pause, he prayed for the young man lying at Mill Row, a stranger to them all, and for his people, far away, wherever they might be.

David stood with his eyes shut and his hands folded, and under the minister's prayer he said his own.

He prayed for Ma's eyes, which had been bad all week at the frame. He prayed for Da, and for Rebecca, though she was a sore trial to him. He prayed for the sick, because the minister had just been through them and the list was handy. And then, because it had been sick all week, and low, and cold, and fevered, and last night it had gone out altogether, he began without meaning to: *and please let the writing machine*.

He stopped, with his eyes shut, in the middle of the meeting-house.

You prayed for people. You prayed for the sick, and for the ones at sea, and for the harvest, because people had to eat it. But the writing machine was a box. He had said so himself, on Thursday night, in the loft: *It's not somebody. It's a box.* Somebody had made it, the way somebody had made Da's engine, and nobody prayed for Da's engine. Not even Da.

So could you pray for a box?

He didn't know. Rebecca might, but she was at the far end of the pew, beyond Ma, and you didn't whisper in the prayer.

So he prayed for the man instead, harder than he had ever prayed for anything: that he would wake up, and that his people would get him back, and that it wouldn't be long.

And at the end, very quietly, he said he was sorry about the man's box, which he and Rebecca had used up between them on sums, and lines, and dragons, and on a cure that cured nothing. He didn't ask for anything for it. He was only sorry. You could be sorry about anything, he was fairly sure.

The sermon was a long one. In the afternoon, at Sabbath school, David stuck at the third question of the catechism, which he had known since he was seven. At dusk Da went down to see to the boiler fires, and when he came back they had worship, and Da prayed for the stranger and his people.

It was Rebecca's turn for the last milk. David went down the ladder after her.

"It's the Sabbath," she whispered, when she saw him kneel by the bag.

"It's a work of mercy," said David. "Looking in on the sick."

"It's a box. You said so."

"I know I did," said David, and ran the teeth open anyway.

She didn't stop him. When the man had had his milk, David drew the writing machine out of its pocket and set it on the quilt, one hand flat on the bottom, and lifted the lid.

Rebecca had the man's hand ready. David laid the first finger on the little circle, gently, as he had on the first night, when it was the man's go.

They waited.

The candle burned down a finger's breadth. There was no lake, and no mountains, and no welcome. The black glass showed them only what any looking-glass would: the candle, small and bright, and their own two faces bent over it, and behind them, dim, the man in the bed.

And the glass stayed black.
