"There's a bird in it," said David.

He was on his knees before Rebecca could set the cup down by the candle. The singing came from inside the man's bag, under the bed: a tune, bright and tinkling, round and round, like a lark that had swallowed a musical box.

"Birds don't sing in bags," said Rebecca. "It's a musical box."

"Who carries a musical box on his back?"

Neither of them knew. Each time the tune came round, it was louder.

Ma was up the row and Da was at the mill. It was the Sabbath, and the walls on Mill Row were thin, and any minute somebody going by would hear a tune coming out of the Boyds' house that wasn't even a psalm.

"It has to stop," said David, and went under the bed on his stomach.

"It isn't ours! Ma said no hoking!"

"If it's a bird, it'll smother," said David. "It's a work of mercy."

Rebecca had no answer to that, because they were Ma's own words.

He dragged the bag out by one strap. Through the small pocket at the top shone a light, cold and white, like the moon behind a curtain. David found the wee tab, ran the teeth open as he had on the jacket, and put in his hand.

What came out was a slab of glass no bigger than his hand, lit from inside like a window at night, and singing. It shivered in his fingers like a bird, and he nearly dropped it.

"It's a wee glass," he said. "A looking-glass."

"Looking-glasses don't sing," said Rebecca.

You will have guessed what it was. JP had set his phone to wake him at half past five on Saturday morning, for a walk by the sea. As far as the phone knew, it was half past five on Saturday morning, and it was doing its best.

Across the glass, in large white figures, it said *5:30*. Under that were two words, each in its own pale box: *Snooze* and *Stop*.

"Stop," Rebecca read. "Even it's telling you."

David shook it like a watch that wouldn't go. He pressed its corners and its back, feeling for a catch, like a locket's. It sang louder. He wrapped it in the spare pillow and lay on top, and the tune went on inside, muffled and furious, like a bee in a jar, and the pillow-slip glowed.

"It says Stop," said Rebecca. "In big letters."

So, because nothing else had worked, he put his thumb on the word *Stop* and pressed it hard, like squashing a midge.

The singing stopped.

The quiet afterwards was enormous. Somewhere up the row a dog barked once, and thought better of it.

The two words had gone, and under the figures was a line of small writing: *Saturday, October 24*.

"It's Sunday," Rebecca told it, in her correcting voice.

The glass went dark, as if it had been put in its place.

David looked at his thumb. He had pressed a word, and the word had done what it said. He put that by, like a spare bolt in Da's tin.

Back went the wee glass into its pocket, still warm from its singing. But the bag was out now, and the big teeth ran all the way round the top of it.

"There might be a letter," said David. "With his name on. For finding his people."

"It isn't ours."

"I'm only looking."

"Looking is hoking," said Rebecca, "in another body's bag." But she knelt down beside him.

First out came two black cords, each with a tiny flat end of metal like the tag of a bootlace squashed thin.

"Leads," said David. "For a very wee dog."

Next came a smooth white thing the size of a pullet's egg, with a seam round it.

"An egg," said David.

"Eggs don't have seams."

He had his thumbnail in the seam when Rebecca took it off him and put it back.

After that came a little dark blue book, with a gold eagle on it and words in gold: PASSPORT, and UNITED STATES OF AMERICA.

"His papers," said Rebecca. "For letting him into foreign parts."

"His people'll be in it," said David. "Give it to Ma."

"And Ma'll ask where we got it." Rebecca put the little book back, shut, and the leads after it.

Then came two flat packets with pictures of nuts on them. David sniffed one, and his whole face woke up.

"That's his," said Rebecca.

"He's on milk. Dr Hamilton said." And he tore it open with his teeth.

"Chocolate," he reported, with his mouth full, "and something like sawdust." He ate it all anyway, to be sure, and pushed the wrapper into his pocket. It was silver inside and not paper at all, and it crackled.

Rebecca put the other packet back before it went the same way.

The black cloth was still strapped to the bag. David lifted one corner. Inside the fold it was black and glassy, ruled across with fine silver lines, like a slate ruled for sums.

"It's for lying on," he said. "On wet grass."

"Oilcloth," said Rebecca, as if she'd thought so all along.

Under the cloth was a fat pocket with teeth of its own. When David went for its tab, she slapped his hand.

Right at the bottom of the bag, in a padded pocket, was a book, longer than his forearm and as black and heavy as Ma's griddle. It had no title, no clasp and no pages, and it was smooth metal all over.

"It's not a book," said Rebecca. "It's a writing-box. Like the Master's."

The Master at school had one that opened on a hinge into a sloping desk. David found the hinge along the back, got his fingernails under the front edge and lifted.

The lid came up and stayed where he left it. Its underside was all one sheet of glass, as dark as the river at night. Below it, where the desk should be, were rows of little square keys with letters on them.

Then the glass lit up by itself.

David sat back so fast that he hit his head on the bedpost.

On the glass, in white letters, it said *Welcome, JP*.

"J. P.," said Rebecca. "Joseph Patrick. The name on his card, that went round his face. Ma said it was the engraver's." She looked at the bed, and went pink. "It was his name all along."

"It's saying welcome to him," said David. "It's his box."

Under the words was a wee picture in fine curving lines, round and round, like the grain of wood about a knot. Rebecca held up her own finger beside it.

"It's the end of a finger," she said. "Look. The lines."

David pressed a letter. Nothing happened. He pressed a great many letters, and nothing went on happening. He rubbed the glass with his cuff, in case the trouble was dirt. That made smears.

That was when he noticed the key in the top corner. It sat apart from the others, smooth and shiny like a chip of the glass itself, and instead of a letter it had a little circle on it, with a stroke through the top like a door left ajar.

It was a fingerprint reader. David didn't know that. But there was a picture of a finger on the glass, and a key made of the same glass, and he was not a boy to leave two and two lying about without putting them together.

He didn't press it. He laid his finger on the little circle, flat, as the picture showed. The glass said:

> Couldn't recognize your fingerprint. Try again.

"Finger-print," Rebecca read out. "That's the mark a finger leaves on a window." She looked at his smears. "You've left plenty."

"It looked at my finger," said David, "and it didn't know it."

He tried again, because it said to. Rebecca, who had said twice that it wasn't theirs, tried hers. It said the same to both.

"It knows a finger," said Rebecca slowly. "One finger. Like the collie at the mill gate knows Da's step, and barks at everybody else's."

They looked at *Welcome, JP*. Then they looked at the bed.

"It's stealing," said Rebecca.

"It's borrowing," said David. "He'll get it back."

He set the writing-box on the quilt and drew the man's right hand out. It was warm and heavy, and it let itself be led, as a sleeping child's does. David took the first finger, the one you point with, and laid it on the little circle, gently, in case it woke him.

*Welcome, JP* was gone. In its place was a page of writing.

"It knows him," whispered Rebecca.

David put the hand back under the quilt, and patted it, which he hadn't meant to do.

The page was like a long letter with two people writing it. Some of it sat in little boxes over on one side, with no capital to start it and no question mark to end it. The rest answered in proper sentences.

"Ballyclare!" said David. "He was coming here! To his dinner!" Then, shocked and delighted: "And asking about public houses."

"Kids," David read. "Why would they have goats at the dinner?"

"It means children," said Rebecca. "It's a low word."

"Great question," David read, and snorted. "It wasn't."

"Since I'm offline," Rebecca read. "Off the line."

"It's a telegraph," said David. "He wrote to somebody, and somebody wrote back."

He felt all round the box for the wire. There wasn't one, and no room inside for a cat, let alone a man with a pen.

"It says itself it's off the line," said Rebecca. "There's nobody at the other end."

"Then who wrote back?"

Rebecca looked at the page for a long time.

"It reads like a person," she said. "But it'll have been put in beforehand, like the tunes in a musical box."

"Then it'll not know what to say to me," said David. "I'm going to write to it."

"You are not. It's the Sabbath."

"One word's not work. Only the letters are all through-other. Where's the H?"

Rebecca opened her mouth to say it wasn't theirs, and shut it again, and bent over the keys. Her finger went along the top row: Q, W, E.

"It's the type-writer," she said. "The Yankee one."

Last winter Da's paper had printed a picture of it, drawn so plain you could read the letters, in this same daft order. Rebecca had chalked it on the hearthstone to see if it made sense. It hadn't, and Ma had made her scrub it off.

"Middle row," she said. "Sixth along. And the long bar at the bottom was bare in the picture too. That'll be for the gaps."

He pressed the H with one finger. A small *h* appeared, not on the page, but in a long box at the bottom of it. With Rebecca calling out the rows, and his tongue between his teeth, he wrote *hello*.

They waited. Nothing happened.

David bent close and said "Hello" to it out loud, politely, in case it was shy.

"It can't hear you," said Rebecca. "It's a box."

At the end of the middle row was a key bigger than the rest, with a little hooked arrow on it and a word: *Enter*. A word, like *Stop*. David got out his spare bolt.

"Enter's 'come in'," he said. "So you press it to let the word in." It made as much sense as anything had that evening, so he pressed it.

The word *hello* jumped up out of the box onto the page. Under it, writing began to come, a word at a time, quick and smooth, as though a hand on the far side of the glass were writing it out:

> Hi there! 👋 How can I help you today?

David felt it all the way down to his boots. It had written to *him*. Nobody could have put that in beforehand.

In among the words was a wee yellow hand, held up, with tiny lines beside it, as if it were waving. It was like the printed hand on a handbill, except that this one wasn't pointing anywhere. David thought it was the best thing he had ever seen.

Rebecca didn't. "Hi there," she said, "like a carter roaring at his horse. And waving. It doesn't even know us." She looked at the beautiful glass and the beautiful writing, and her mouth went small, the way Ma's did. "A grand thing like that, and no more manners than Sam Orr."

In the bottom corner of the glass, very small, were more figures: *5:52 AM*, and under them, *10/24/2026*.

"A.M.," said Rebecca, who read the notices in Da's paper. "That's morning."

David looked at the window. It was as black as the bag. "And two thousand and twenty-six," he read, and laughed. "That's the printer again."

"Let me," said Rebecca. "I'll ask it what day it is, properly, with a big letter and a question mark. Then we'll see."

David watched her hunt along the rows for a big W. There was only the one, and it came out small. She put the gaps in with the long bar. The question mark had a key of its own, on top of a slanting stroke, and when she pressed it the stroke came out instead.

*what day is it/*

Rebecca looked at it with dislike. Then she looked up at the man's questions, and her face changed.

"He couldn't do it either," she said. "Not one question mark, nor a big letter to start. It's not just us." She frowned. "Only Ballyclare gets a big B. It keeps them for places."

She pressed Enter herself.

> It's **Saturday, October 24, 2026**, just before 6 AM. You're up early! Is there anything I can help you plan for the day?

"It's Sunday," said Rebecca. "It's Sunday, and it's night, and it's eighteen eighty-one."

"It's a long way out, for an error," said David, in her voice. She didn't laugh.

"It's been asleep in his bag all day, the same as him," she said. "It's lost count."

A thing that could lose a day could lose a year or two, David supposed. "Or a hundred and fifty-five," he said, having done the sum.

"A hundred and forty-five," said Rebecca.

So it was wrong about the day, and the hour, and the year, which is a great deal to be wrong about all at once. But it wrote.

"What is it, though?" said Rebecca. "A type-writer only writes what you give it."

David looked at the glass, and the writing, and the wee waving hand.

"It writes," he said. "It's a writing machine."

That was when they heard Ma's step on the row.

Rebecca shut the lid, as you'd shut a book, and the glass went out. David pushed the writing machine into the bag, ran the teeth round and shoved the lot under the bed with his foot. By the time the door opened, Rebecca was sitting by the bed with the cup and the spoon, and David was on the floor, looking at nothing.

Down there in the dark, the writing machine slept, like a fire banked for the night.

Ma came in with the face she kept for burnt porridge.

"Did he take his milk?"

"He did," said Rebecca, which was true, if nobody asked when.

"She allows he's not drunk," said Ma, untying her bonnet. "She says he's a Yank, and that's as bad."

When Da came home from the mill, with the dry cold on his coat, they had worship, as they did every Sabbath night: a chapter, a psalm with Da raising the tune, and Da's prayer. Every time David moved, the wrapper in his pocket crackled. Ma's eyes were shut, but her eyebrows heard it.

Da prayed for the stranger under their roof, and for his people, who would be watching the road for him. David thought of the little blue book shut in the bag, and kept very still.

Up in the loft, in the cold and the dark, Rebecca whispered, "We're not to touch it again."

"No," said David.

"It isn't ours."

"I know."

He lay a long while, listening to the man's breathing come up through the boards, until he slept.

Some time towards morning, in its pocket, the wee glass burned down like a candle and went out. Nobody heard it go.

At half past five the mill horn sounded, which was what the wee glass had been trying to do all along. The mill horn, at least, knew what day it was.

It was Monday, which meant the Master, and the slate, and the sums. David was no good at sums. He lay in the dark and thought about them, and then about a box under the bed that wrote back.

"Rebecca," he whispered. "Do you think it could do sums?"
