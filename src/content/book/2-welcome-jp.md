"There's a bird in it," said David.

He was on his knees before Rebecca could set the cup down by the candle. The singing came from under the bed, from inside the man's bag: a bright, tinkling tune that went round and round, and came back louder every time.

"Birds don't sing in bags," said Rebecca. "It's a musical box."

"Who carries a musical box on his back?"

Neither of them knew.

Ma was up the row at Mrs Strahan's, and Da was at the mill. It was the Sabbath, and the walls on Mill Row were thin, and any minute somebody going by would hear a tune coming out of the Boyds' house that wasn't even a psalm.

"It has to stop," said David, and went under the bed on his stomach.

"It isn't ours! Ma said no hoking!"

"If it's a bird, it'll smother," said David. "It's a work of mercy."

They were Ma's own words, and there was no arguing with those, even second-hand.

He dragged the bag out by one strap. A cold white light was shining through the small pocket at the top. David found the wee tab, ran the teeth open as he had on the jacket, and put in his hand.

What came out was a slab of glass no bigger than his hand, lit from inside and singing. It shivered in his fingers like a caught bird, and he nearly dropped it.

"It's a wee glass," he said. "A looking-glass."

"Looking-glasses don't sing," said Rebecca.

You will have guessed what it was. JP had set his phone to wake him at half past five on Saturday morning, for a walk by the sea, and as far as the phone knew, it was half past five on Saturday morning, and high time he was up.

Across the glass, in big white figures, it said *5:30*. Under them were two words, each in its own pale box: *Snooze* and *Stop*.

"Stop," Rebecca read. "Even it's telling you."

David shook it. He pressed its edges and its back, feeling for a catch, as if it were a locket. It sang louder. He wrapped it in the spare pillow and lay on top, and the tune went on inside, muffled and furious, and the pillow-slip glowed.

"It says Stop," said Rebecca. "In big letters."

So, because nothing else had worked, he put his thumb on the word *Stop* and pressed it hard, like squashing a midge.

The singing stopped.

The quiet afterwards was enormous. Somewhere up the row a dog barked once, and thought better of it.

The two words had gone. Under the figures was a line of smaller writing: *Saturday, October 24*.

"It's Sunday," Rebecca told it, in her correcting voice.

The glass went dark, as if it had been put in its place.

David looked at his thumb. He had pressed a word, and the word had done what it said. He put that by, like a spare bolt in Da's tin, in case it came in handy.

Back went the wee glass into its pocket, still warm from singing. But the bag was out now, and the big teeth ran all the way round the top of it.

"There might be a letter," said David. "With his name on. For finding his people."

"It isn't ours."

"I'm only looking."

"Looking's hoking," said Rebecca, "in another body's bag." But she knelt down beside him.

Two black cords came out first, each with a tiny flat end of metal, like the tag of a bootlace squashed thin.

"Leads," said David. "For a very wee dog."

Next came a smooth white thing the size of a pullet's egg, with a seam round its middle.

"An egg," said David.

"Eggs don't have seams."

He had his thumbnail in the seam when Rebecca took it off him and put it back.

There was a little dark blue book, too, with a gold eagle on the front and gold words: PASSPORT, and UNITED STATES OF AMERICA.

"His papers," said Rebecca. "For going into foreign parts."

"His people'll be in it," said David. "That's the very thing we're looking for."

"And it's shut," said Rebecca. "You'd not open a letter to see who it was from." She put the little book back, and the leads after it.

"I would," said David.

"You would not."

David found two flat packets with pictures of nuts on them. He sniffed one, and his whole face woke up.

"That's his," said Rebecca.

"He's on milk. Dr Hamilton said." And he tore it open with his teeth.

"Chocolate," he reported, with his mouth full, "and something like sawdust." He ate it all anyway, to be sure, and pushed the wrapper into his pocket. It was silver inside and not paper at all, and it crackled.

Rebecca put the other packet back before it went the same way.

The black cloth was still strapped flat to the back of the bag. David lifted one corner. Inside the fold it was black and glassy, ruled across with fine silver lines, like a slate ruled for sums.

"It's for lying on," he said. "On wet grass."

"Oilcloth," said Rebecca firmly.

Under the cloth was a fat pocket with teeth of its own. When David went for its tab, she slapped his hand.

Right at the bottom of the bag, in a padded pocket, was a book. It was longer than his forearm and as heavy as Ma's griddle, and it was black metal all over, smooth and cool, with the corners rounded off. It had no title and no clasp, and when David turned it to look at the pages, there weren't any.

"It's not a book," he said.

"It's a writing-box," said Rebecca. "Like the Master's at school."

The Master's writing-box opened into a sloping desk, and it was locked with a little brass key that he kept on his watch-chain. So David looked for the keyhole. There wasn't one on the front. Along one side were two little slots, flat and narrow, and he put his eye to one and saw nothing but dark.

"There's the keyhole," he said. "For a very flat key."

"Then he has the key," said Rebecca, "and it's locked, and that's the end of it."

But a locked box still has a lid, and a lid has to meet the rest somewhere. David ran his thumbnail all round the edges. There was a seam, but it went right round without a break, and there was nothing his nail could lift. Four little black feet sat on top, soft to the thumb, one at each corner.

"You have it upside down," said Rebecca. "Feet go at the bottom."

He turned it over. Now the seam ran round only three sides. Along the fourth, at the back, the top and bottom were joined by a long, stiff hinge, and in the middle of the front edge was a shallow dip, as if somebody had pressed a thumb into the metal while it was soft.

David got his nails into the dip and lifted. The whole thing came up off the floor with his fingers, top and bottom together, like a mussel that won't let go.

"Hold the bottom," he said.

Rebecca held it flat to the boards with both hands. David lifted again. This time the top came away from the bottom, stiffly at first and then smoothly, swinging up on the hinge at the back, and when he let go, it stayed exactly where he had left it.

The underside of the lid was all one sheet of dark glass. Below it, where the Master's desk had green cloth for writing on, were rows of little square keys, black, with a white letter on each.

Then the glass lit by itself.

David sat back so fast that he hit his head on the bedpost.

It was a picture, and it filled the whole glass: a lake among mountains, with snow on the tops and the water bluer than any water had a right to be. It was lit from behind. Rebecca had gone quiet, the way she did when she was drawing something in her head.

"It's a magic lantern," said David, rubbing his head.

He had seen one the winter before last, when a missionary came to the schoolhouse with pictures of India and a lantern that smelt of hot oil. The pictures had come out on a sheet, round and wavering, and every one had been coloured by hand, as bright as this. But a magic lantern needs a lamp, and the lamp has to be behind the picture. David felt the back of the lid. It was cool. He looked behind it and under it and along the hinge. There was no lamp and no smell of oil, and no sheet either, and the picture didn't waver at all.

Across the lake were big white figures, like the ones on the wee glass, and under them, in words, *Saturday, October 24*.

"It's Sunday," Rebecca told it.

She said it with less hope than she had said it to the wee glass. They had come out of the same bag, David thought, so of course they told the same story.

What David really wanted was the keys. He pressed the D, for David.

The picture went up. It slid off the top of the glass all in one piece, lake and mountains and Saturday together, and behind it was the same lake gone misty, as if it had been breathed on. In the middle of the mist was a round grey picture of a head and shoulders with no face at all, like a likeness somebody had cut out of paper and forgotten to finish. Under it, in white letters, it said *JP*. And under that was a wee picture of fine lines going round and round inside one another.

"I did that," said David. "With the D."

Rebecca was reading the letters under the faceless head. "J.P.," she said. "Da's paper puts that after a magistrate's name."

David looked through the door at the shoes on the kitchen hearth.

"Him? In them?"

"Maybe in America they don't mind," said Rebecca doubtfully.

David didn't think so. The engine at the mill had a brass plate on it with the names of the men who made it, and Da polished it every Saturday whether it needed it or not. "It's the maker," he said. "J.P. and Company. They put their names on, so you'll know who to blame."

The round-and-round lines were no help to either of them. David rubbed at them with his cuff in case they were a smudge, but they were under the glass and stayed. "It's a knot," he said. "Like in a board."

"It's the rings in the lade when you throw a stone in," said Rebecca.

David pressed the D again, to see the next picture. Nothing happened. He pressed the A, and the V, and the I. Nothing went on happening.

"It's only got the two," he said.

His eye went to a key in the far corner, above all the letters.

It sat apart from the others, and it had no letter. It was smooth and shiny, glassier than the rest, and on it was a little circle with a stroke standing up through the top, like a clock with only the one hand, and that at twelve.

David remembered his spare bolt: he had pressed a word on the wee glass, and it had done what it said. This key had no word on it, but it was the odd one out, and the odd one out is always the one that does something.

"Leave it," said Rebecca.

He pressed it.

The glass went black.

There was no dimming about it. One moment the misty lake was there, and the next there was nothing, as quick as a nipped wick, and the room was small and yellow again round their own candle.

"You've broke it," whispered Rebecca.

David's hand went out to the letters, and Rebecca caught it by the wrist. "Don't you touch another thing on it."

So he bent down and blew on the black glass, softly, as Ma blew on the coals of a morning. It did nothing at all.

"You've broke the man's box," said Rebecca, "and him not even awake to see it broke. Put it away. Put it back in the bag, and maybe it'll mend."

She shut the lid herself, gently, as if there were somebody asleep in it.

David couldn't leave it like that. Before she could get it into the bag, he had his nails in the dip again, just to see how bad it was, and lifted.

The glass lit. There was the lake, and the mountains, and the Saturday, as bright as ever.

For a moment neither of them said anything.

"It lights when you open it," said Rebecca at last. "Like a musical box. Lift the lid, and it plays."

"And that key puts it out," said David, with respect.

"Then don't you touch it."

"I'm not touching it. I'm minding it." And he laid his finger over the little circle, flat and light, to keep it covered, the way Da kept a hand on a valve he didn't trust.

The picture went up by itself.

There was the misty lake again, and the faceless head, and *JP*, and the knot. And under the knot was new writing:

> Your fingerprint couldn't be recognized. Try using a different finger.

"I never pressed it!" said David.

"You were touching it."

"I was minding it!"

"Finger-print," Rebecca read out, frowning at the word. "That'll be finger-marks."

Ma was forever at them about finger-marks on the window. David looked at his finger. It had been in the bag, and on the passport, and in the chocolate.

"Recognised," he said, offended. "As if it ever saw my finger before in its life."

"It wants a different one," said Rebecca. She looked at his. "Yours is clatty."

She wiped her own first finger on her apron until it squeaked, and laid it on the little circle, lightly, the way David had, and nowhere near pressing.

The writing blinked, and came back, word for word the same.

"So it's not dirt," said David, delighted.

"Not with mine," said Rebecca.

David held up both hands with the fingers spread. "Which, then? There's nine more."

"They're all clatty. You had every one of them in that chocolate."

"Then give it another of yours."

"I will not." She put her hands under her apron. "It's turned me away once. I'll not stand at its door twice, like a beggar."

David looked at the words again. *Your* finger-print, it said. Not this finger or that one: *yours*. So it was a matter of whose go it was, like marbles, where you shoot once, and if you miss, the next boy shoots. He had had his go, and Rebecca had had hers.

Ma was up the row at Mrs Strahan's. Da was at the mill.

They both looked at the bed.

"No," said Rebecca. "It's stealing."

"It's borrowing," said David. "He'll get it back. And it's his go."

He set the writing-box on the quilt beside the man and drew the man's near hand, the right one, out from under the covers. It was warm and heavy, and it let itself be led. David took the first finger, the one for pointing, and laid it flat on the little circle, gently, not pressing at all, because pressing put it out.

Rebecca held up the candle and watched, tight-lipped.

The faceless head went away, and the mist with it. In their place was a page of writing, black on white, and across the top, in large letters, it said *Welcome, JP*.

David put the hand back under the quilt, and patted it, which he hadn't meant to do.

"It knew him," whispered Rebecca.

"It was only fussy," said David. "It liked his better."

"It knew him. The way the collie at the mill gate knows Da's step, and barks at everybody else's."

"It never barked at us."

"It told us to try a different finger," said Rebecca. "That's barking, for a box."

David bowed to the bed. "Welcome, J.P.," he said. "Welcome home, your worship."

"Hush," said Rebecca, and looked at the man's face, in case.

The page was like a long letter with two people writing it. Some of it sat in little boxes over to one side, with no capital to start and no question mark to end. The rest answered, in proper sentences.

*is it rude to bring wine to dinner in Northern Ireland*

> Not rude at all! 🍷 Wine is a thoughtful, common gift for hosts in Northern Ireland. If they have kids, a small treat for them is a lovely touch too.

A red glass of wine was painted in among the words, like a printer's ornament.

"Ballyclare!" said David. "He was coming here. To his dinner." He read on. "And asking after public houses. A magistrate!"

"Kids," he read. "Why would they have goats at the dinner?"

"It means children," said Rebecca. "It's a low word."

"Great question," David read, and snorted. "It wasn't."

"Since I'm offline," Rebecca read. "Off the line."

"Like the telegraph," said David. "He wrote to somebody, and they wrote back down the wire."

He felt all round the writing-box for the wire. There wasn't one. There wasn't room inside it for a cat, let alone a man with a pen.

"There's no wire," said Rebecca. "There's nobody at the other end."

"Then who wrote back?"

Rebecca looked at the page for a long time.

"It writes like a person," she said. "But it'll have been put in beforehand, like the tunes in a musical box. All you get out is what was set in."

David wasn't listening. At the foot of the page was a long empty box, and the keys were right there under his hands. He pressed one in the middle, for luck.

A small *h* appeared in the long box. Not on paper. On the glass.

He looked at it. Since he had an h, it seemed a pity to waste it, so he went looking for the rest of a word he was sure how to spell.

"Where's the E? They're all through-other."

"It isn't ours," said Rebecca, and leaned over his shoulder to look. Her finger went along the top row without touching. Q, W, E.

She stopped.

"It's the type-writer," she said. "The Yankee one."

Last winter there had been an advertisement in Da's paper for a machine that wrote like print, with a picture of it drawn so plain you could read the letters on the keys, in this same daft order. Rebecca had copied it onto the hearthstone in chalk, row by row, to see if it made sense. It hadn't, and Ma had made her scrub it off.

"Top row, third along," she said.

With Rebecca calling out the rows, and his tongue between his teeth, David wrote *hello*, one finger, one letter at a time.

They waited.

A type-writer, Rebecca said, printed on paper, the way the press prints a newspaper. So David waited for the paper. He looked behind the lid for it, and underneath, and along the sides. There wasn't any. The *hello* sat in its box on the glass and did nothing whatever.

"Maybe it didn't take," said David.

"In the picture," said Rebecca, "the long bar at the bottom had no letter on it. That'll be for the gaps."

David put a gap with the long bar, and wrote it again, harder.

*hello hello*

Nothing.

"It's the Sabbath," said Rebecca, rather late.

"It's not work if it's no use," said David.

And it was no use, that was plain. A type-writer that wouldn't print was only a lot of keys. You couldn't post what it wrote, or keep it, or show it to the Master. It was no better than writing in the steam on the window.

"Put it away," said Rebecca, "before Ma's back."

She took it off the quilt to do it, with her left hand under the bottom and her right round the far end, her thumb across the big hooked key at the end of the middle row for a grip, and lifted.

The *hello hello* jumped out of its box and up onto the page, where it sat in a little box, over to one side, like the man's.

And under it, writing began to come. It came a word at a time, quick and smooth, as though a hand on the far side of the glass were writing it out:

> Hi there! 👋 How can I help you today?

Rebecca nearly dropped it. David caught the bottom, and between them they got it down on the floor without a word.

"It's printed it," said David. "My hello. It was only slow." He saw the rest. "And it's writing by itself!"

In among the words was a wee yellow hand, held up, with three tiny strokes beside it. It was like the printed hand on a handbill, only this one wasn't pointing at anything. David thought it was the best thing he had ever seen.

Rebecca didn't. "Hi there," she said. "Like a carter roaring at his horse. And its hand up at us. It doesn't even know us." She looked at the beautiful glass and the beautiful writing. "A grand thing like that, and no more manners than Sam Orr."

"How can I help you today," David read.

"That's what the draper says to anybody that comes in the door," said Rebecca. "It's another of its pieces. It plays what's in it."

"But what set it going?"

Rebecca had lifted it, and it had started. So she lifted it again, just as before, her thumb in the same place. Nothing came. David gave it the wee shake Da gave his watch when it stopped, and nothing came of that either.

"It's played its piece," said Rebecca. "It'll want winding."

What had set it going, they didn't find out that night.

David hunted all over it for a winding-hole. What he found instead, very small in the bottom corner of the glass, were more figures: *6:04 AM*, and under them, *10/24/2026*.

"A.M.," said Rebecca, who read the notices in Da's paper. "That's the morning."

David looked at the window. It was as black as the bag.

Rebecca frowned at the strokes between the figures, as she did at the draper's tickets. "That's no price," she said, and left the ten and the twenty-four alone.

"And two thousand and twenty-six," said David, and laughed. "That's the printer again." And, in Rebecca's own voice: "It's a long way out, for an error."

She didn't laugh. "It's been asleep in his bag all day, the same as him," she said. "It's lost count."

A thing that could lose a day, David supposed, could lose a year or two. "Or a hundred and fifty-five," he said, having done the sum.

"A hundred and forty-five," said Rebecca.

So it was wrong about the day, and the hour, and the year, which is a great deal to be wrong about all at once. But it wrote.

"What is it, though?" said Rebecca. "A type-writer only writes what you give it."

David looked at the glass, and the writing, and the wee yellow hand still held up.

"It writes," he said. "By itself. It's a writing machine."

Ma's step came down the row.

Rebecca shut the lid, like the cover of a book, and the glass went out. David pushed the writing machine back into its pocket, ran the teeth round, and shoved the whole bag under the bed with his foot. By the time the door opened, Rebecca was sitting by the bed with the cup and the spoon, and David was on the floor, looking at nothing.

Ma came in with the face she kept for burnt porridge.

"Did he take his milk?"

"He did," said Rebecca, which was true, if nobody asked when.

"She allows he's not drunk," said Ma, untying her bonnet. "She says he's a Yank, and that's as bad."

When Da came home from the mill, with the dry cold on his coat, they had worship, as they did every Sabbath night: a chapter, and a psalm with Da raising the tune, and Da's prayer. Every time David moved, the wrapper in his pocket crackled. Ma's eyes were shut, but her eyebrows heard it.

Da prayed for the stranger under their roof, whose name the Lord knew though they didn't, and for his people, wherever they were, who would be watching the road for him tonight.

David had his eyes open a crack, which they were not supposed to be. So he saw that Rebecca's were open too, wide, and fixed on nothing, as if she had just found something she'd lost.

Up in the loft, in the cold and the dark, she whispered, "David. He's not a magistrate."

"Who isn't?"

"J.P. When I mark a shirt for somebody, I put on their letters. Never mine." He heard her turn over. "Joseph Patrick. It was on his card, round his face, and Ma said it was the engraver's. It was never the engraver's. It's his."

David thought of the brass plate on Da's engine. So the letters on the box weren't the maker's after all. They were the man's own, like the name on a dog's collar.

"We can't tell Ma," he said.

"No." To tell Ma the man's name, they would have to tell her where they had found it.

"We're not to touch it again," Rebecca whispered.

"No," said David.

"It isn't ours."

"I know."

He lay a long while, listening to the man's breathing come up through the boards, until he slept.

Some time towards morning, in its pocket in the bag, the wee glass burned down like a candle and went out. Nobody heard it go.

At half past five the mill horn sounded, which was what the wee glass had been trying to do all along. The mill horn, at least, knew what day it was.

It was Monday. Monday meant the Master, and the slate, and the sums, and David was no good at sums. He lay in the dark and thought about them, and then about a box under the bed that wrote by itself.

"Rebecca," he whispered. "Do you think it could do sums?"
