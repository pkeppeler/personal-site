What David said to the Master, Rebecca heard in the yard, in the rain, with the coal shovel in her hand.

He came in at the back gate late, with his cap dripping and his eyes on his boots.

"Ma's had your tea on the hob this hour," said Rebecca. "What did he want?"

"He asked who was helping me."

"And what did you say?"

David looked at the hedge. The rain ran off the end of his nose.

"David."

"I couldn't say a box under the bed, could I?" he said. "He'd have had it off us before the bell. So I said my sister."

Rebecca set the shovel down in the coal bucket, carefully, as you set down a thing you would otherwise throw.

"It's near enough true," said David, very fast. "You found me the letters, the first night. And you knew about the colour."

"I said colour had a u in it!"

"I know. Only he asked where you'd got a Yankee spelling, so I said out of our uncle's letters."

Rebecca couldn't speak at all.

"He said, 'Rebecca Boyd. I might have known,'" David hurried on. "He said you'd the best head for figures he ever had in the fifth class, and it was a crying shame you were ever taken out of it."

That went in somewhere under Rebecca's ribs, and stayed.

"And you're to show me how, and not do them for me. And he says to tell you," David finished, since he had started, "that colour has a u in it, whatever they do in America."

Rebecca picked up the bucket and went into the house without a word, which was worse than any word, and David knew it.

All the rest of that wet afternoon she sat at the flowering frame beside Ma and was furious in small, neat stitches. She was furious that David had told a lie in her name, and more furious that it was nearly true. She was furious that the Master thought she spelt like a Yank. And under all of that, where she didn't care to look, was the crying shame.

The light went early. Ma sat back and pressed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "That's us," she said. "I can't see the needle, never mind the work."

By bedtime the fury had worn down to something smaller and harder, like a stone in a shoe. If she was to have the blame of the writing machine, she thought, she might as well have the use of it.

It was her turn for the last milk of the night. Ma and Da were already in the settle bed, and Da was snoring before she had the cup filled. Rebecca took the candle into the room, with her old school copybook under her arm, and pulled the door to behind her.

She gave the man his milk a drop at a time, and waited for every swallow, and he took it all.

"It's only a lesson," she told him.

The writing machine lay under the bed beside the bag, where David had left it. She had never lifted the lid herself. On the first night it had taken the two of them: David's nails in the dip at the front, and her hands holding the bottom flat to the boards, or the whole thing came up at once like a mussel. Now she did both halves, a hand to each, and the lid came up and stayed where she left it.

The glass lit, and there was the lake among its mountains, with the big figures across it, and under them *Tuesday, October 27*. It was Wednesday. It had lost its day on Sunday, and never found it since.

The man's right hand lay on her side of the bed, the door side, and it let itself be led. She laid his first finger flat on the little circle, as she had watched David do, and the lake slid away, and there was the page, with *Welcome, JP* across the top. At the foot of it was David's composition, with its colour that had no u. Rebecca gave it the look she usually kept for David.

Warming the milk, she had thought what she would give it. You put in the start of a thing, David said, and it wrote you the rest. At the hob that had seemed easy. Now, with the letters under her fingers, every start she could think of was a foolish one. She could start it on a sum, but she could do sums. She could start it on a composition, and it would only spell colour wrong again.

In the end she gave it the start of what she missed.

She set it out the way David had put in his composition: what kind of a thing it was to be, and then what it was on. Row by row, with one finger and the long bar for the gaps, she wrote:

*a lesson in what a girl of thirteen should know*

She pressed Enter, and the writing came.

> What a great idea! Here's a quick lesson on what a 13-year-old girl might be learning today:
>
> - **Online safety:** never share personal details with strangers, and always be kind online!
> - **Coding:** try a beginner-friendly language like Scratch or Python!
> - **Wellbeing:** your mental health matters just as much as your physical health.
> - **Your future:** it's never too early to explore careers in science, engineering, medicine, or the arts!

It ran on below that, down past the bottom of the glass, to somewhere she couldn't follow. She went over what she could see, slowly.

*Online*, she supposed, was *on the line*, since the writing on Sunday had said *offline*, and that was off it. Which line, it didn't say. If it meant the railway, every child in Ballyclare had been told to keep off that, not how to be safe on it. If it meant the telegraph, she couldn't see how a girl would be unkind to a wire. The bit about strangers Ma could have told it for nothing.

There was a language, it seemed, called Python, which was a snake, and another called Scratch, which was what hens did. She could not think what a girl would say in either. And a girl's mental health mattered just as much as her physical health, as if a girl's mind might take a chill.

Then she came to the last line, and kept coming back to it.

Science. Engineering, which was Da's sort of thing. Medicine, which was Dr Hamilton's. The arts, which was drawing, or might be. *It's never too early.*

Rebecca looked at the first finger of her left hand, the one that lived under the frame. It was rough at the tip with needle-pricks, and had been since she was twelve.

"It's a Yankee lesson," she told herself, in Ma's voice. "For Yankee girls."

That was very likely so. It didn't help as much as it should have.

She opened her copybook at a clean page, to copy it out, and wrote ONLINE SAFETY across the top in her best hand.

And there she stuck. She didn't know what to put underneath.

After a while she gave it up, and started it on something she knew she would like.

*a poem about a dragon*

> **The Sleeping Dragon** 🐉
>
> Beneath the hill where the bracken grows,
> A dragon sleeps with his tail round his nose.
> His scales are green as the sea in spring,
> And folded tight is each leathery wing.
>
> The village children tiptoe near
> To hear him breathing, year by year.
> But nobody knows when the dragon will wake,
> Or how many winters his dreaming will take.

"Oh," said Rebecca, out loud, and put her hand over her mouth.

It was lovely. The second time through, she found she was listening to the man breathing in the bed beside her.

The machine had drawn a dragon of its own at the top, green and no bigger than her thumbnail, and it looked like a lizard that had been told a joke. Rebecca could do better than that, and she did. In the margin, beside the heading with nothing under it, she drew him with her stub of pencil, curled up with his tail round his nose, every scale laid over the next like petals in a flowering pattern. She drew him asleep.

While she drew, the glass grew dim, and went dark.

Her pencil stopped. Then she remembered the wee glass on Sunday, going dark by itself the moment she told it the day, as if it had been put in its place. Glass out of that bag did that, it seemed. And she had seen the lid bring this one back. She finished the dragon's tail first.

When the dragon was done, she sat a while watching the man.

Every time his finger opened the machine, it said welcome to him. It knew him. It was the only thing in Ballyclare that did. And if it knew him, it would know his people, and their names, and where they lived; and a letter couldn't so much as begin without a name.

She shut the lid and lifted it again, as she and David had between them on Sunday, and the glass lit on the lake. She fetched the hand, and there was the welcome, and at the foot of the page she wrote:

*a letter to the family of jp to say he is safe and well looked after*

> Of course! Here's a short letter you can personalize:
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I'm writing to let you know that JP is safe and well looked after. Please don't worry. I'll be in touch again soon with more news.
>
> Warm wishes,
> [Your Name]

Rebecca went through it word by word, to be fair to it.

It began *Dear*, as a letter should, and then, where the name ought to be, it said *Name*, shut in between two little square hooks, such as Da's paper put round FROM OUR REPORTER. At the foot, where the letter wanted signing, it said *Your Name*, the same way.

What it meant, she knew from the agent's dockets: a word printed small, and a space after it for whoever's name it was, and you filled them in yourself. It had written her a blank, and left the names to her.

So it didn't know his people at all. It didn't know him either, not really. It knew his finger, as the collie at the mill gate knew Da's step, and could no more have told you who he was.

She searched along the foot of the glass, in case there was anything else of his. There was a row of little pictures there, and one of them she knew at once: an envelope, folded and sealed. On its corner was a round mark, where a postmark goes, and in the mark was a number.

*12*

The twelfth, then, the day it was posted, as a postmark gives the date. Or the number of his house, written on the front with his name, like the number on his card. Either way, it was a letter, and it had come for him.

Somebody had been writing to him. Somebody was watching the road for him, as Da had prayed, and their names would be inside, and where they lived.

Her finger had gone out to it before she knew. She took it back, and put her hand in her lap, and held it there with the other one.

It is a hard thing to leave shut what might tell you everything. Rebecca managed it. She had told David herself, over the passport, that you'd not open a letter to see who it was from. This one was sealed, and it was his.

So was the bag. It lay half out from under the bed, with the black cloth strapped across its back, and under the cloth was the fat pocket she had slapped David's hand away from on the first night. If he had his people's direction written down anywhere, it would be in there.

She put her hand flat on the cloth, over the pocket, and kept it there until the wanting went off. It was only the oilcloth, and it was keeping the pocket shut.

Before she shut the lid she noticed, because she noticed most things, a small number at the far end of that row of little pictures, beside the figures for the hour. It said *24*, and after it came an odd mark, a wee nought and a slash and another nought, as if somebody had begun a sum and thought better of it. The slash was the draper's stroke, near enough; but knock off the noughts, as she had on the man's card, and there was no price left at all. It meant nothing to her.

She shut the lid, and the glass went out, and she took her dragon up the ladder to bed.

On Thursday it rained all day, and David came home with six more sums and a look at Rebecca that was half sorry and half hopeful.

"You're to show me how," he reminded her. "The Master said."

"Then I will," said Rebecca, which was not the answer he had been hoping for.

She took the man his milk, and David came after her with his slate. While the man swallowed, a drop at a time, she did the first sum in the slate's corner, step by step, as the Master had once done them on the board for her. David watched the pencil the way a dog watches a stick that hasn't been thrown yet. Then he did the second himself, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth, and got *216*.

"Put it in," he said. "Only to see."

Rebecca had done that one too, small, at the very edge of the slate, under her thumb. She found she wanted to know what the machine would write, a good deal more than she meant to let on.

"One," she said. "Then you'll do the rest yourself."

David drew the writing machine out from under the bed and lifted the lid, and the lake came up. Rebecca fetched the hand, and there was the page. At the foot of it was last night's blank letter, and David's whole face lit.

"You were at it last night, on your own! Who's that to?"

"Nobody," said Rebecca. That was the trouble with it.

He laid two fingers on the smooth square below the letters and drew them towards him, and the writing ran back down the glass to her poem. "A poem! About a dragon!"

"It was a lesson first," said Rebecca, going red.

"A dragon lesson."

"Do your sum."

He hunted the figures along their row, one at a time.

*divide 8136 by 36*

> 8136 divided by 36 is **226**. ✅
>
> Want to try another one? 😊

Rebecca lifted her thumb. *226*, said the edge of the slate, in her own small figures.

David looked from the glass to the slate and back. "It's the same as yours."

"It is," said Rebecca. She was pleased, and then cross with herself for it, since by rights it was the machine that had got *her* answer.

David was rubbing out his 1 with a licked finger when the box came.

It slid up out of the corner of the glass by itself, and sat there, and the whole glass went dimmer, like a lamp with its wick turned down.

"It's marking me wrong," said David, injured. "And me rubbing it out!"

But there was no cross in the box. There was a wee picture: a long narrow shape drawn in white lines, lying on its side, with a knob at one end and a sliver of red at the other. Beside it were two lines of writing.

*Battery low*

*20% remaining*

"It's a wee coffin," said David.

"It is not." But it was, very nearly. "Coffins don't have knobs."

"That's the handle."

"Hush," said Rebecca, and looked at the bed, and David hushed. You didn't say coffin in a sickroom.

*Battery low.* The second word she knew better than she wanted to. Dr Hamilton said it on doorsteps up and down the row, with his hat in his hand and his voice gone quiet: *She's very low. He's low today.* Nobody ever asked him what he meant.

The first word she knew only a piece of. Once, at the railway, Da had lifted her up to see the telegraph in the station office. He had wanted her to see the needle that talked, flicking left and right all by itself. What she remembered was a shelf under the counter, with a row of glass jars on it and wires coming out of their tops, and the clerk saying, "Mind, now. Don't be touching the battery."

"A battery's jars," she said. "At the telegraph. In a row, with wires."

"It's guns," said David at once. "A battery's big guns. In a row, on wheels. Soldiers have them."

They looked at the writing machine. It had no jars and no guns. It lay on the quilt, flat and quiet, with its glass gone dull.

David lifted it an inch, in both hands, and tilted it gently one way and then the other, with his head on one side, listening for the slop of jars or the roll of shot.

"You'll break it," said Rebecca, and took it off him.

"I won't." But nothing had slopped, and nothing had rolled.

Rebecca pointed, without touching, at the corner of the glass. The wee number beside the hour had changed. It said *20* now, with the same odd mark after it as the twenty in the box.

"It said twenty-four last night," she said.

"Twenty-four what?"

"I don't know." She looked at the coffin, and at the word *low*.

"Copy it in," said David. "The box's words, like a sum off my slate. Then see what it writes after."

She did the writing herself. She was quicker at the letters than David, and he knew better than to say so. She took the top line of the box, as it stood:

*battery low*

> Looks like your battery is getting low! Your **battery** stores energy so your device can run without being plugged in. 🔋
>
> When your battery percentage gets low (around **20%**), just plug your charger into a wall outlet to recharge. Easy!

It began by giving them the box's words back, only longer, which was what David did at school when he didn't know the answer.

Beside the first line was another wee picture. This one was green all through, and it stood upright, with a little knob on its lid.

"There," said Rebecca. "A jar."

"A coffin," said David. "Stood on its end. And this one's full."

"David!"

But she had already stopped listening to him. In the middle of the writing was a word she knew, or nearly knew: *percentage*. Near it stood *20*, with the odd mark after it.

Per cent. She had done pages of it in the fifth class, in the interest sums: five per cent, five pounds in every hundred. And here was the word, sitting beside the twenty and its mark, so the mark must be the machine's short way of writing it, as the grocer wrote *lb* for pounds.

"It's per cent," she said. "Twenty in the hundred."

"Twenty in the hundred what?"

In the interest sums it was always pounds. Here it said *your battery percentage*, and a battery was jars, or guns, and this had neither.

"Of its battery, whatever that is," said David. "Put that in, then. *Battery percentage.* See what it writes after."

Rebecca looked at the coffin, and the glass gone dull behind it. She had sat in enough sickrooms with Ma to know what you did when somebody was low. You didn't keep them talking.

"No more writing," she said. "Not today."

"Then read me the rest."

"*Your battery stores energy.*" Rebecca frowned. "Energy's what Ma says Mrs Strahan has too much of."

"So it keeps Mrs Strahan in it." David looked at the writing machine with a new respect.

"*So your device can run without being plugged in.*" A plug was a bung, for a barrel or a boat, and *run* made no more sense than the rest. She went on. "*Just plug your charger into a wall outlet to recharge. Easy!*"

"Easy," she said again, in the voice she had kept for *Hi there*.

But David had sat up straight.

"A charger's a horse," he said. "A war-horse, for charging. And a battery's guns. Don't you see? It's a battle. *Recharge*, that's charge again. The guns go off, and the horses charge, and then they go back and charge again."

"It's a writing-box," said Rebecca, "not Waterloo."

"And a wall outlet," said David, and stopped. His face changed. Rebecca knew that face, and didn't like it.

The lade came out under the mill through an arch in the wall and ran away down to the river. David knew every stone of it. His own wee wheel turned in the lade, and at the end of a day the mill carters led their horses down to the outlet, to stand in the shallows and drink.

"That's where you water a horse," said David. "At the outlet in the wall, where the lade comes out. You take your charger down after the battle and let him put his nose in, and then he's fit to charge again. It says so."

"It says *plug*. You don't plug a horse."

"You plug his nose in the water."

"And he never came here on a horse," said Rebecca. "He came on his feet. In those shoes."

David's eyes went from the coffin to the word *low*, and then to the writing machine, lying dim on the quilt. Rebecca could see the horse in his face before he said a word: a horse that had been worked too hard.

"Then it means itself," he said. "It's tired out. It wants watering."

"It does not."

"I could take it down before school, when there's nobody about. I'd only hold the corner of it in."

"You'll not take it within a field of the lade," said Rebecca. "Nor the river, nor the rain-barrel, nor the jug on the washstand. You don't put water near a writing-box. Ink runs, and paper rots, and anything with a hinge on it goes green."

"There's no paper in it."

"There's no horse in it either."

That was the end of the watering.

While they argued, the glass had dimmed and gone dark, and the box had gone with it. David sat looking at the black glass.

"It's counting backwards," he said. "Twenty-four, and then twenty. It's an alarum. When it gets to nought, it'll go off, like the wee glass." He was quite sure of it at once.

He turned the writing machine over in his hands, while Rebecca held her breath, and looked all over the underside for the bell. There were four wee feet of something like india-rubber, and a great many tiny screws, and no bell anywhere.

Rebecca laid her ear against it. "Clocks tick," she said.

"Maybe it's a quiet one."

Rebecca let him keep his quiet alarum. But when the box came, the glass had gone dimmer, and she had seen a lamp do that at the end of a long night, with the oil nearly done. She bent and sniffed along its edge. It smelt of nothing at all, not paraffin, not even the man's bag.

"There's no oil in it," she said, more to herself than to him.

It wanted no water, and no oil, and it had no bell. What the machine had meant was a socket in the wall, of course, and there was not one in all of Ballyclare, nor would be for a great many years.

"What are you two at in there?" called Ma, from the frame.

"Sums, Ma," said Rebecca. There had been one.

She shut the lid, very gently, and slid the writing machine back under the bed.

That night, up in the loft, she lay and listened to the man's breathing come up through the boards, slow and even, as it always did.

She had watched Dr Hamilton take the man's wrist and look at his watch, counting. She didn't know what he counted, or what number he hoped for. She knew only that a count could go the wrong way, and that when it did, he said *low* on the doorstep.

"David," she whispered. "Are you asleep?"

"No."

"It's like the doctor counting," she said. "A count that goes down, and *low*, and a coffin. It's like when somebody's bad."

David was quiet.

"What happens when it gets to nought?" he said.

Rebecca didn't answer. She didn't know, and she wasn't going to guess out loud, not over the man's head.

Mrs McCready would have called the wee coffin a sign. Ma would have told her not to be talking nonsense. Rebecca lay in the dark a long while, trying very hard to be Ma about it.

"We'll let it rest," she said at last. "Tomorrow nobody touches it. Not you, not me. That's what Ma does when somebody's bad. She keeps the room quiet and lets them sleep."

"It's not somebody," said David. "It's a box."

"Then it'll not mind resting."

On Friday it rained, and nobody touched the writing machine. Rebecca saw to that. Twice David drifted towards the room door, and twice she looked at him across the frame, and he drifted away again and did his sums at the kitchen table, on his slate, the long way. He got three of them right, and Rebecca showed him where the other three had gone wrong, since the Master had said.

Late that night, with the last milk, she lifted the lid. She only meant to look in on it, as you look in on someone sick last thing, with the candle shaded in your hand. But the glass lit on the lake, and the lake wanted the finger, and she fetched the man's hand, and after the welcome, there in the corner was the box again: *Battery low*, and *8% remaining*, and the coffin in it had hardly any red left at all.

The number beside the hour said *8*.

David had come down the ladder behind her in his stocking feet, and for a long time he said nothing.

"Nobody's touched it," he whispered. "Not since yesterday. Not once."

"I know."

It had lain shut and quiet a whole day and a night, as good as any patient, and it was lower than ever.

They looked at the eight, and the odd mark after it, and the coffin with next to nothing in it. In the bed the man slept on, as if there were all the time in the world.

"Rebecca," said David. "How long is eight per cent?"
