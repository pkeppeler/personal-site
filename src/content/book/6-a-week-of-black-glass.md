Some weeks are over before you have properly begun them. Others take a month to get from one Sabbath to the next, and you could swear somebody had slipped in extra days while you weren't looking. The week after the writing machine went black was one of those.

It began in the dark, at the mill horn, with Rebecca sitting straight up in the loft.

She had remembered in her sleep. The writing machine was still lying open on the quilt below, where David had left it on Sunday night, with its black glass turned up to the ceiling. And Ma was always first into the room in the morning, with the man's milk.

Rebecca went down the ladder in her stocking feet, missing the rung that creaked. She didn't need a candle. Her hands found the quilt, and the man's knee under it, and then the cold flat edge of the metal. She shut the lid, and it made no more noise than a book. She slid the writing machine into its padded pocket at the bottom of the bag, ran the teeth shut, and pushed the bag back under the bed against the wall.

She was on the bottom rung again when the kitchen door opened, and Ma came in with the candle and the cup.

"You're early about," said Ma.

"I heard the horn."

Ma looked at her over the candle a moment longer than she needed to. Then she went to the man, and Rebecca climbed back up to the loft and lay with her heart going.

Monday was washing day on Mill Row. But that Monday the rain came down past the window in long grey strings, and Ma stood watching it, and pushed the basket back under the table.

"There'll be no drying in that," she said. "Nor tomorrow either, by the look of it."

David came down last and ate his porridge as if somebody were making him count it. He didn't go round by the lade to see to his wheel, which he had done every morning since he built it, rain or no rain. He went straight up the road to school with his hands in his pockets, and didn't look back.

"What ails him?" said Da.

"Growing," said Ma, and gave Rebecca the look again.

That night was Hallowe'en.

In other years it had been the best night of the autumn. Mrs McCready would come in after tea with red apples in her apron and a fistful of hazelnuts for the hob, and stories that Ma didn't hold with, and afterwards nobody liked going up the ladder in the dark.

This year she came all the same. But first the man had to be turned.

Ma and Rebecca turned him every night and every morning, from his back to one side and then to the other, so that the bed wouldn't wear sores into him. Ma took his shoulders and Rebecca took his knees. Mrs McCready, who could lift little heavier than a teapot these days, held the pillow ready and gave orders.

"Over you go, son," she told him. "Easy, now. You're a brave weight for a man that lives on milk."

He went over without a murmur, and his breathing never changed. Rebecca gave him his milk on that side, a spoonful at a time, waiting for every swallow, and wiped his chin.

Then they sat round the fire with the apples. The nuts went on the hob and cracked, and Mrs McCready picked them off with her fingers, which had no more feeling in them than horn.

David said he didn't want an apple.

Ma looked at him as if he had said he didn't want his legs. In all his eleven years David had never once refused an apple, and on Hallowe'en least of all. Mrs McCready said nothing. She put one in his hand anyway, and he sat on the low stool with it in his lap and didn't eat it.

"Did I ever tell you," said Mrs McCready, "about my mother's cousin's man, out the Ballynure road?"

"You did," said Ma.

"Not the weans, I didn't." She settled her shawl. "He took a sleep on him at the harvest, and they couldn't waken him. Not with shouting, nor shaking, nor a feather held under his nose. So the doctor said he was away, and they washed him and laid him out in his good shirt, and the whole townland came to the wake. And at two in the morning, with the kitchen full and the tea going round, didn't he sit up in the bed and look about him, and say, 'Who's dead?'"

David laughed out loud before he could stop it, and went red to the ears, and put his face down to his apple.

"Mrs McCready," said Ma. "With the man lying there."

"Sure he can't hear me," said Mrs McCready. "And if he can, it'll give him something to look forward to." She cracked a nut. "He lived to be ninety. He said it was the best sleep ever he had."

Her eyes went through the open door to the bed, and stayed there, and when she spoke again it was quieter.

"Under a thorn, Sam Orr says you found him. On a Sabbath morning." She shook her head. "I'd not have touched him. Not under a thorn."

"You'd have carried him home on your own back," said Ma, "and well you know it."

Mrs McCready thought about that, with a nut halfway to her mouth. "I would, aye," she said. "But I'd not have liked it."

Rebecca had picked haws off that very thorn since she was big enough to reach them, and nothing out of the way had ever happened to her there. Until the Sabbath before last, said a small voice at the back of her head, and she told it to hush.

When Mrs McCready had gone, David sat at the table with his slate and the six sums the Master had set, and did nothing to any of them.

Rebecca sat down beside him. "Give it here, and I'll show you the first."

"I don't want showing."

"The Master said I was to."

"I don't care what the Master said." He sat with the slate pencil in his fist until the candle was half down. Then he went up the ladder, and in the morning the six sums went to school with nothing under them at all.

Tuesday was darker still. Ma sat at the frame with the work so close to the window that her nose was nearly in the muslin. She held her needle up to the grey light and poked the thread at it, and missed, and missed again, and in the end she handed it to Rebecca without a word. This was Ma, who by Da's account could thread a needle in the dark.

Later she took the agent's docket off the dresser, to see how many pieces were due to Mr Crothers on Monday. She held it close, and then out at arm's length, and gave that to Rebecca too.

"Read me that."

Rebecca read it out, the pieces and the price. The price never went up.

Ma nodded. "It's the light," she said.

It had been the light all week, and the week before. After dinner Ma gave Rebecca the veining of the leaves to do, the finest work in the whole pattern, which Ma had never given to anybody.

That evening David did his sums by himself, at the far end of the table, with his arm curled round the slate so that nobody could see. Rebecca could see anyway. The figures were wandering off like sheep through a gap in a hedge, and he wasn't even going after them.

On Wednesday David didn't come home at dinner-time.

Ma glanced at the door, and at the pot, and wrapped a farl in a clean cloth. "Run up and see what's keeping him."

Rebecca knew before she got there. The town children had gone home for the break, and two wee ones from out the country sat in the porch eating their bread, and stared at her. Through the open door she could see the long room, and the map of Ireland on the wall, and the Master at his high desk with his spectacles on. On the front form, the bench nearest the Master, sat David by himself with his slate.

She had sat on that form herself, a year ago. It looked smaller.

The Master looked up. "Rebecca Boyd," he said. "Come in. You may as well."

She came as far as the end of the form. David didn't look round. Every one of the six sums on his slate had a line through it, in the Master's hand.

"Last week," said the Master, "your brother brought me long divisions without a blot, set out in steps the like of which I never taught in this school. Then for a day or two there were small neat figures in the corner of his slate, in a hand I'd know anywhere." He hooked his thumb into his watch-chain, beside the little brass key of his writing-box. "And these two days, not one right. Not one."

His eyes came to Rebecca, over the tops of his spectacles.

"The help has stopped, I see."

It wasn't fair. She had shown David how, as she was told, in her own hand where anybody could see it. She had offered to show him every night this week. It was David who wouldn't be shown. And she couldn't say one word of it without telling on him, here, in front of the Master, with David beside her gone red to the back of his neck.

"Yes, sir," said Rebecca.

"He may have his dinner when he has one of them right."

She set the farl down at the end of the form, by David's elbow. He didn't touch it. As she went out past the country children in the porch, she heard the slate pencil begin again behind her, slowly, with a squeak.

All the way home she argued with the Master, and won every time. It was no use at all, since she hadn't said any of it.

"He thinks it was me," she said that night, in the dark of the loft. "Doing them for you."

"I know."

"I never did them. I showed you."

"I know." The quilt rustled. "I got one right, after. He let me go."

Below them the man breathed in, and out, and in again, and the rain went on.

"It's not the sums," said David at last.

Rebecca waited.

"I used it up. The man's box. On sums, and lines, and Belfast." His voice had gone thick. "And when he wakes, it'll be lying under the bed black, and that'll be me that did it."

"We both did."

"I did the most."

Rebecca reached across in the dark and found his quilt, and pulled it up over his shoulder. He let her.

Dr Hamilton came on Thursday, with the rain running off the brim of his hat.

He sat on the edge of the bed with the man's wrist in his fingers and his watch in his other hand, and counted. He had them turn the man, and examined his back where the bed pressed on it, and nodded at what he didn't find there.

"You'd put a hospital to shame, Mrs Boyd."

Ma took that as she would take a parcel she hadn't sent for. "How long, Doctor?"

He was a while answering. He put his watch away, and then took it out again, as if it might know.

"There's no knowing," he said. "It might be the morn. It might be a good while yet." He looked at Ma's face, and Rebecca could see him hunting for something to give her. "Thon Turville girl woke after nine years."

"Nine years," said Ma.

She saw him to the door, and thanked him, and came back and put the kettle on, because there was always something to be done about a kettle.

Rebecca stood at the foot of the bed with the cup in her hands. She had the best head for figures the Master ever had in the fifth class, and she couldn't help doing the sum.

In nine years she would be twenty-two. David would be twenty, and taller than Da, and out at the mill. Ma and Da would have slept nine winters in the settle bed. And somewhere, far away, the man's people would have been watching the road for him nine years.

The man lay with his face to the ceiling, in no hurry at all.

On the first Sabbath, when the doctor hadn't said when, she had wanted to know. Now somebody had told her, and she would have given it back if she could.

She didn't tell David.

That night, when the frame was covered, she took a handful of old dockets from the dresser drawer, where Ma kept them for lighting the fire, and drew on their backs. Rebecca always drew when she couldn't think, and some nights when she could think too much.

She drew a dragon asleep with his tail round his nose, as she had in her copybook. Then she drew leaves coming down on him: one autumn's worth, and the next, and the next. By the ninth autumn there was no dragon left to see, only a heap of leaves with the tip of a tail sticking out at one side.

She didn't draw any more that night.

Mill Row was the last row in the town. The lane past it went to the mill and the river and nowhere else. All the same, on Friday a great many people found they were just passing.

Mrs Kane from the far end of the row was just passing with a jug of buttermilk. Two girls from the bleach works were just passing arm in arm, and giggled at the man's shoes on the hearth, and then, from the room door, at the man, until Ma looked at them. Sam Orr's mother was just passing, and told the whole kitchen how heavy the man had been to carry, though it was Sam who had carried him.

And last of all, in her good black on a weekday, came Mrs Strahan, who had never been just passing anything in her life.

She stood at the foot of the bed and considered the man a long while, as she might a loaf she suspected of being yesterday's.

"Soft hands," she said. "He never did a day's work with those."

"He'll be a gentleman, then," said Ma.

"Or a man that plays cards for money." Mrs Strahan's eye travelled down, as eyes will, to the corner of the bag where it showed under the bed. "And that's his pack. You'll have been through it."

"I have not," said Ma.

Rebecca discovered that the man's pillow wanted shaking. She shook it thoroughly, standing exactly in front of the bag.

"You'd want to know what a man carries," said Mrs Strahan. She lowered her voice, though the man could have heard nothing if she had shouted. "A Yank. They say there's Yanks going about with gunpowder in their bags."

"They say a great deal," said Ma. "It's not ours to look in. Nor anybody's."

There was no gunpowder in the man's bag. Rebecca was nearly sure.

Mrs Strahan sniffed. She left a tract on the chair by the window for when he woke, all about the wickedness of drink. At the door she turned back, and tucked the quilt in at his feet where it had worked loose, quick and hard, as if somebody had done it wrong on purpose.

"My sister in Larne's no better," she said, to nobody in particular, and went.

That night at the fire Da took out his watch and wound it, with the wee key that hung on its chain. David, at the table, watched every turn of the key.

On Saturday, after dinner, Ma went in next door with the soap and the towel to wash Mrs McCready's hair for the Sabbath, and Rebecca sat at the frame on her own, doing the veining.

From the room came a sound like a length of calico being torn in two.

She was through the door with her needle still in her hand. David was on his knees under the window, with the man's bag dragged out into the grey light and the black cloth fastened to its back. Along one edge of the cloth ran the short black cord she had noticed on the riverbank. One end of it went into the cloth at the corner, through a wee pocket, and was fast there. The other hung loose, with a flat tip of metal like a bootlace tag squashed thin. And in David's fist was a little black strap.

"You've torn it!"

"I haven't. Look." He laid the strap back over the cord and pressed it down flat, and it held. Then he pulled, and it came away again with the same ripping noise, and nothing was torn at all. "I only took hold of the cord, and the strap came away like that. Twice now."

Rebecca took the strap from him. It was sewn to the edge of the cloth, and it had no buckle and no button. One side of it was rough, and caught at her thumb. The other side was as soft as felt. Press the two together, and they clung.

"Burrs," she said.

Every autumn the collie at the mill gate went about with burrs in his coat that took a comb to get out, and her own stockings came home from the lanes stuck all over with them, every burr a wee ball of hooks. These were burrs too, only so fine that she couldn't see a single hook, and tidy, and somebody had grown them on purpose.

"What are you at?" she said.

"Looking for the key." David sat back on his heels. "It's locked. It opened for his finger before, and now it'll not open for anything. Not even him. So it's locked properly now, like the Master's box, and the Master keeps his key on his chain." He looked up at her. "You said it yourself, the first night. He has the key."

"I said it was locked, and that was the end of it."

"Well, it's locked," said David. "So the key's somewhere."

He held up the loose tip of the cord.

"Like those things in the bag," said Rebecca. "For your very wee dog."

"It's a key. On a chain. Tied on, so it'll not get lost."

He had the wee glass out of the top pocket already. It lay black in his hand. He pressed it where the words used to come, and along its edges and its back, and it didn't so much as flicker.

"It's gone," he said. "The same as the other."

Rebecca couldn't tell. The wee glass had gone dark by itself before, the moment she told it the day was Sunday, and nobody had lit it since or knew how. So this might only be that dark, the waiting kind. Or it might be the black that had come over the writing machine a week ago, when it went to nought.

At the foot of the wee glass was a slot, flat and narrow, like the two in the side of the writing machine. David held the flat tip to it and pushed, gently. It went in, and stopped.

They watched the wee glass. It stayed black.

David pulled the cord free again, and put the wee glass back in its pocket at the top, and brought the writing machine up out of its padded pocket at the bottom. He tried the slits along its back first, and Rebecca caught his wrist.

"Not there. That's where it breathes."

So he turned its side to the window. There were the two slots he had once put his eye to and seen nothing but dark. The cord was just long enough. He put the flat tip to the nearer slot, and it went in like a key into a lock.

He tried to turn it, as Da turned the key in his watch.

"Don't you force it," said Rebecca.

It wouldn't turn. She made him take it out, to see he hadn't bent it, and turned the tip over in her fingers. It was smooth and flat, and the same on both sides. When she gave it back, David put it in again without looking, and only afterwards did she see he had it the other way up. It had gone in just as sweetly. No key Rebecca had ever met would do that.

What he had fitted into the slot was the charging lead of a solar panel. I should like to tell you that it woke.

Nothing happened.

David lifted the lid. The glass was black, and gave them back the grey window and their own two heads. He shut it again. The rain went on against the panes, and the writing machine lay across his knees with the cord from the black cloth running into its side, as still as the man in the bed.

"It's not the right key," said Rebecca.

"It fits," said David. "It fits the both of them." He followed the cord back to the cloth with his finger, and Rebecca saw a thing come right in his face. "Then the cloth's the machine's too. It's its case. That's why the key's tied on."

"It's oilcloth."

"Oilcloth to keep it dry, then. Like a cover on a book." He laid his hand on the folded cloth. "It fits. So that's where it goes."

Rebecca might have argued. A week ago she would have. But she had no better notion herself, and a key left in its lock did no harm to anybody.

So David laid the writing machine back in its padded pocket with the cord still in its side, and ran the teeth shut as far as the cord would let them, and pressed the wee burr strap down along the edge of the cloth. He was pushing the bag back under the bed when he stopped, and put his hand flat on the floor by the wall.

He took it back wet.

"It's soaking under there."

The damp had come up through the floor along the back wall, as it did in a wet November, and the bottom of the bag was cold and dark with it. Rebecca thought of ink running, and paper rotting, and hinges going green.

"Not on the floor," she said.

They put the bag up on the deep sill of the room window instead, where it was dry, with the black cloth folded against the panes and the cord running in under the teeth.

When Ma came back from Mrs McCready's, with her sleeves still wet to the elbow, she stopped in the room door and sniffed.

"What's that bag doing up there?"

"It was wet under the bed, Ma," said Rebecca. "The floor's soaking along the wall."

Ma bent and put her nose to the bag, and took it away again quickly. "Wet! It's more than wet. It smells like the bottom of the lade." She looked round the room: at the man's jacket, which had hung on the back of the door nearly a fortnight, and at the sheets, and the blankets, and the man himself. "It all does," she said. "The whole house."

But she left the bag where it was.

Late that night the rain stopped. Da went to the back door to look at the sky, and stood there a while with the cold coming in round him, and said the wind had gone round to the north and there were stars over the mill chimney.

Ma came and stood beside him in the doorway. Then she turned back to the kitchen, where the washing that should have been done on Monday stood heaped in its basket under the table, higher than ever.

"If Monday's fine," she said, "I'm washing everything in this house, and that man's things too."
