The wind stayed in the north all Sunday, and in the night the frost came down. On Monday morning the yard was white from the back step to the hedge.

Ma stood in the doorway in the half-dark, with her shawl over her head, and looked at it.

"It'll lift," she said. "It'll be off by nine."

She had spent the whole of the Sabbath not thinking about the washing, which is harder work than washing. Twice in the meeting-house David had seen her eyes go to the window to find out what the sky was doing, and twice she had fetched them back to the minister.

Now the fire was roaring before the horn had gone, and the big pot was on the crook, and the tubs stood ready on the form, the long bench at the foot of the yard. Da had taken his dinner to the mill tied up in a cloth. On a washing day a man was only in the way.

Before the porridge, Ma and Rebecca changed the man's sheets with the man still in the bed. They rolled him one way and then the other, and took his own shirt off him, and put one of Da's on. He slept through all of it.

After the porridge Rebecca put on her boots and set off up the town to Mr Crothers's, with the week's pieces wrapped in a clean cloth and carried in front of her in both arms, as if they might spill.

"Mind he counts them in front of you," Ma called after her. "And if he says the veining's crooked, it is not."

David carried water. He carried it until his arms felt a good inch longer than they had at breakfast, and the pot boiled, and the kitchen filled with steam and the smell of soap, and Ma's sleeves were pushed up past her elbows and stayed there.

By half past nine the sun was up over the mill, and the frost smoked off the hedge. The hawthorn stood black and wet and shining. The wind had dropped to a breeze, the kind that dries anything you give it, and big white clouds hurried over on it, one after another.

Ma came out with the first of the sheets, steaming, and spread them over the hedge. The man's shirt went on the line, at the end, by itself. It was of some stuff so thin that when Ma held it up, the sky showed through it, and she never wrung it at all.

Then she came back for the things that wanted air and not water.

"That's not going in any tub," she said of the man's jacket. It whispered when she shook it, like a lady's silk. "It's silk, or as near as makes no matter, and you don't put silk in a tub. Not if you want it back the same size." So the jacket went on the line beside the shirt, hung by its collar, and shone there in the sun. The shoes went on the back windowsill, where the whole row could admire them.

"And now," said Ma, "that bag."

David was at the back door with his slate under his arm, on his way to school. He stopped.

Ma lifted the bag down off the sill of the room and carried it out by one strap, at arm's length, with her face turned away from it. She set it on the end of the form, in the sun.

She wrinkled her nose at the black cloth strapped to its back. "And you're the worst of the lot."

She worked it out from under its two straps, a corner at a time. It came away stiffly, still folded, and the short black cord came with it, lying along its edge. But the end of the cord didn't come. It ran off the corner of the cloth, and down, and in under the teeth of the bag, and there it pulled tight and stopped her.

Ma frowned. She ran her finger down the cord to the place where it went in under the teeth, and there her finger stopped too.

David stopped breathing.

At the other end of that cord, down in the bag, in its padded pocket, lay the writing machine, with the flat tip in its side where he had put it himself on Saturday. All Ma had to do was run the teeth open.

Ma had opened a great many things in her life: the door to callers, every letter that came from Philadelphia, and Da's waistcoat pockets on a Saturday night. But David had never once known her open anybody else's bag.

"Whatever you're tied to," she told the cord, "that's his business. I'll not start on other folk's bags on a washing day."

She shook the black cloth open instead. It had four leaves, and on the inside every leaf was black and glassy, ruled across with fine silver lines. Ma held it up and considered it.

"That's an odd class of oilcloth," she said.

She licked her thumb and rubbed a corner. "Glazed." She pulled the wet cloth from her apron string and wiped the black side down, corner to corner, until it shone. Then she took it to the hedge glazed side out, to the weather, which any carter could have told you is the right way to hang oilcloth.

She tried it high on the hedge first, and the cord pulled tight between the cloth and the bag. So she hung it low, a hand's breadth above the form, and moved the bag along to sit under it, where the cord lay slack. The metal rings at the cloth's corners caught on the hawthorn as if they had been made for it.

Ma stood back. Among the white sheets the black cloth looked like a hole in the hedge.

"There," she said. "That'll sweeten you."

She turned round, and saw David.

"Are you here yet? School!"

He went. At the corner of the row he looked back. The black cloth hung on the hedge between the sheets, and the whole of the sun was on it.

We must leave it there for the morning, because David had to go to school, and so must we.

The Master set long division. David got three right and one wrong, which was better than last week, and the Master said so, in a voice that meant he would believe it when he saw it twice.

The schoolroom windows faced the street. Every time a cloud crossed the sun, the long room went grey and then light again, and every time, David found he was looking at the window.

"Boyd," said the Master. "Are you expecting somebody?"

"No, sir."

He wasn't. He was only thinking about Ma's hands, and the teeth of a bag.

When the Master let the town children out at dinner-time, David ran.

He went round by the backs, because it was quicker, and because from there you could see over the hedges into every yard. All Mill Row had its washing out, white on every hedge and line. Theirs had a black square in the middle of it.

Rebecca was coming along the backs from the town end, with a new bundle of muslin under her arm and a look on her face as if she had swallowed a pin.

"He kept me standing the whole morning," she said, before David could ask, "and counted them twice. And then he said Ma's veining was the best he'd had off Mill Row in a twelvemonth."

"It's your veining."

"I know whose veining it is."

They came in at the yard gate together. From the kitchen came the clank of the pot lid, and Ma's voice, telling a sheet what she thought of it.

The bag sat on the form under the black cloth, and the cord ran from the corner of the cloth straight down into its teeth, just as Ma had left it.

David only meant to see that his key was safe. He knelt by the form and put his eye to the gap in the teeth where the cord went in.

There was a light in the bag.

It was down at the very bottom, where no light had any business to be. It was small, and white, and very bright.

David's heart turned over. He thought of the shawl, and the smell of hot wool, and the writing machine too hot to hold.

"It's burning!"

He tore the teeth open and plunged his hand in, to snatch the machine out before the man's bag went up. He had it by the corner before he understood that nothing was hot. The bag was as warm as a loaf from lying in the sun. But the metal under his fingers was cool, and there was no smoke, and no smell at all.

He lifted the writing machine out onto the form, minding the cord. The light was still shining. It came from the side of the machine, right beside the slot where the cord went in: a wee white light, no bigger than the head of a tack, with no heat in it whatever.

Rebecca was kneeling beside him by then. "It was dead," she said. "We saw it."

"Hedgehogs," said David. His voice came out odd. "I said. They wake up."

"In the spring," said Rebecca. "It's November."

But she said it under her breath, and didn't take her eyes off the light.

David looked at the cord, going in by the light. "It's the key," he said. "It took. Like a live coal when you put it in among the dead ones. It only wanted time."

"It had all Saturday," said Rebecca. "And all Sunday, on the sill. You lifted the lid, and it was as black as your boot."

So the key had been in all along, and done nothing, and today something else had happened. David couldn't think what.

"Lift the lid," said Rebecca.

He put his nails in the dip and lifted it.

The glass lit.

There was no lake. The glass stayed black all over but the middle, where there was a little picture in white: the wee coffin again, lying on its side, with its knob at one end. It was nearly empty. Through it, from corner to corner, ran a crooked stroke of lightning. Under it were a figure and the odd mark:

*7%*

"Seven," Rebecca whispered.

Then the glass went black again, all at once.

"It was at nought," said David.

"It went to one, and then to nought, and it's been at nought a week and more." Rebecca was counting on her fingers, which she had never needed to do in her life. "And now it's seven."

It had gone up.

In all the time they had known it, the number had only ever gone down. It went down when they wrote, and when they didn't. It went down by the fire, and in the dark press, and resting, and shut. Nobody had ever seen it go up. They hadn't known it could.

Something that had been sitting on David's chest for a week got up and went away.

"There's lightning in the coffin," he said.

He thought of Da's paper, the summer before last, when lightning came down on a byre out the Doagh road and killed a cow. *Struck by the electric fluid*, the paper said. Da had read it out at the fire.

"It's been struck," said David. "Like the Doagh cow."

"Struck dead," said Rebecca, "and gone up to seven?"

David chewed his lip. "Da says lightning's electricity," he said instead. "The same stuff as goes along the telegraph wire, only wild."

"The telegraph has a battery." Rebecca said it slowly. "The jars, with the wires out of their tops."

"The paper said fluid," said David.

He lifted the writing machine in both hands and tilted it, gently, one way and then the other, and listened, as he had listened once before for the slop of jars. Nothing slopped.

"It's not that kind of jar, then," said Rebecca. She looked at the black glass where the lightning had been. "But it's in there somewhere. Electricity. In its battery, whatever its battery is."

"Where would it get electricity?"

"I don't know." She looked up at the sky, which was blue, with the white clouds going over. "There's been no thunder since the harvest."

David shut the lid, and lifted it again, to be sure. The little white picture came back, and the lightning, and the *7%*.

"What's that light?" said Ma.

She was standing at the back door with the basket on her hip, and she was looking straight at the glass.

Nobody said anything. Ma set the basket down on the step and came across the yard, wiping her arms on her apron.

"Is that a lamp you have lit in that man's bag?"

Before either of them could answer, she put her hand out flat over the glass to feel the heat off it, and held it there, and found none. There was no lamp behind the glass and no flame in it, only the lightning in the middle. Then the glass darkened by itself, and Ma took a step back from it.

She glanced over the hedge.

In the next yard the sheets were flapping, and beyond them more sheets, and beyond those half Mill Row was out in its yards with its sleeves rolled up.

"Shut that," said Ma, very low.

David shut the lid.

Ma took a wet sheet from the basket, shook it out with a crack, and pegged it on the line between the form and the rest of the world. She turned a bucket upside down, sat on it, and folded her arms.

"Now," she said. "The pair of you."

Rebecca told her all of it, first to last, and David put in the bits she got wrong. The singing in the bag. The wee glass that said *Stop*. The writing-box, and the lake, and the little circle.

"And it wouldn't open for anybody," said Rebecca, "but the man's finger."

"His finger," said Ma.

"Only laid on, Ma," said David. "Not pressed. Pressing puts it out."

"You took that poor man's hand to it, and him lying there not able to say yes or no."

"I said it was stealing," said Rebecca.

"It was only borrowing," said David.

"It was a liberty," said Ma. "Go on."

"It writes, Ma," said Rebecca. "You put in the start of a thing, and it writes you the rest. It did David's sums."

"Sums," said Ma, and David could tell that was no news to her. Then she sniffed. "A box that writes. By itself. Then it can do the veining, and I'll sit at the fire like a lady."

"It did my lines too," said David. "And Belfast. And a poem about a dragon." Rebecca stood on his foot.

"And then the number went down," said Rebecca, "and down, and we didn't know what to do for it. So we..."

She stopped.

"Warmed it," said David. "At the fire. In the shawl."

"So that's what the shawl was," said Ma.

"It was cold, Ma. We thought it was cold."

"And then we put it in the press," said Rebecca, "in the dark, to rest."

"With you leaping up to fetch blankets," said Ma to David. "Up to something. I said so at the time."

"And on the Saturday night it went to one," said David, "and then to nought, and the glass went black and stayed black."

"And you down the ladder at the horn on the Monday," said Ma to Rebecca, "to put it away before I came in with the milk."

Rebecca looked at her boots. "Yes, Ma."

Ma sat on her bucket a while without a word. The sheet on the line between them and the row filled with wind and emptied again.

"And now it's lit," she said at last.

"Seven," said Rebecca. "Seven per cent."

"Of what?"

Neither of them could tell her.

David was looking at the cord again, and thinking. Something was different today, and the plainest thing different was the sun. The bag had been as warm as a loaf under his hand.

"It's the sun on it," he said. "Warming it. Like the shawl, only gentle."

He took off his jacket and spread it over the writing machine, to keep the sun off, and lifted one corner to look. The wee white light shone on in the dark under the jacket, as steady as before.

"It's not that, then," said Rebecca.

He took his jacket back. And the wind took the hedge.

It caught the end leaf of the black cloth, flipped it back over its neighbour, glass to glass, and held it there.

"It's out!" said Rebecca, with her eyes on the light.

The gust dropped. The leaf fell open again.

"It's back." She looked up, along the cord, at the cloth on the hedge. "It went out when the cloth shut. It's the cloth, David."

David was up before she had finished.

"Don't you lay a finger on that oilcloth," said Ma. "I've only got it sweet."

She said it, though, in a voice that wanted to see what he would do.

David took hold of the far edge of the black cloth and swung it across, glass to glass, till it was shut from end to end.

"Out," said Rebecca.

He opened it. For a breath, nothing happened.

"Back."

Ma got up off her bucket and came to see for herself, and they were all three bent over the form when the yard went grey.

A cloud had come over the sun, a big one with a dark belly, going fast on the wind. Its shadow slid over the sheets and the hedge and the black cloth, and nobody had touched a thing.

"It's out," said Rebecca.

David laid his hand flat on the black cloth. It was still warm from the morning.

The cloud passed on over the mill. The sun came back on the yard, and the sheets went white again.

"Back," said Rebecca.

"It's not the heat," said David slowly. "The cloth's as warm as ever."

"Then it's not the warm of the sun it wants," said Rebecca. "It's the light of it."

Ma watched the wee white light for a long while, with the look she gave the man when he took his milk well.

"It's feeding," she said.

David followed the cord, from the cloth to the machine, and saw that it had never been a key at all.

"It's the spoon," he said.

Ma gave him a look. But she didn't tell him he was wrong.

Then she looked up at the hedge, at the black cloth she had hung there herself that morning, and down at her own two hands, red from the tubs, as if they had gone and done something without asking her.

David already had his hand in the top pocket of the bag.

"What are you at now?" said Ma.

"It fitted the wee glass too, Ma. On Saturday. I tried it."

The wee glass was as black as it had been on Saturday. He laid it on the form and drew the cord out of the writing machine's side, and Rebecca made a small noise, and the wee white light went out at once. He put the flat tip into the slot at the foot of the wee glass.

The wee glass lit almost before he had let go.

It wasn't the whole glass. It was a small white picture in the middle of the black: a wee coffin of its own, with lightning in it, and a number underneath.

"It's not dead either," said David.

"That was quick," said Rebecca. "The other took all morning."

"It's not as particular."

They had hardly done looking at it when the wee glass darkened again. A word came up on it in white letters, a word that meant nothing to any of them, and was gone. Then, all by itself, the wee glass lit up properly, from edge to edge. There was a picture behind, of nothing at all: blue and purple and pink run into one another, like the oil that floats on the lade below the mill. There were the big white figures. And under the figures, in smaller writing:

*Sunday, November 8*

"Sunday," David read out. "November the eighth."

"It's Monday," Rebecca told it, in her correcting voice.

"Sunday!" said Ma.

She looked round at the yard: at the sheets on the hedge, and the shirts on the line, and the tubs steaming on the form, and over the hedges at the rest of the row, with its washing out for anybody to see.

Rebecca bent closer. Up in the top corner there was something more, so small that she had to put her nose nearly to it.

"*No service*," she read out.

"Sunday, and no service," said Ma. "Whoever reared that thing never reared it right."

"It said Saturday, the night it sang," said David. "And that was the Sabbath."

"Then it's a day behind," said Ma, "and proud of it."

Rebecca had gone quiet. She was looking at the wee glass on the form, and then at the writing machine beside it, and then away through the back door, across the kitchen, to the door of the room, where the man lay asleep in the bed.

"They're his, Ma," she said. "The both of them. The writing machine'll not open for anybody's finger but his. We tried ours."

Ma looked at the bag, and at the black cloth on the hedge, for a long moment.

"Then it's the man's," she said, "and we'll mind it for him."

Down at the mill, the horn sounded for the men's dinner.

Ma was on her feet. "That's one o'clock! Your school!"

"But Ma..."

"Your school. You're late as it is." She put a cold potato into one of his hands and his cap into the other. "And you," she said to Rebecca, "give the wee one its feed first, since it's quick, and then put that string back in the man's machine, just as you found it. And then come and help me wring."

The last David saw, as he ran, was Rebecca kneeling at the form with the wee glass in her lap, and Ma standing over her in the sun with her arms folded, watching it feed.

He was late, and had to stand by the Master's desk through all the reading lesson for it. He didn't mind in the least. Every time the window darkened, he thought, *Out*. And every time the sun came back, *Back*.

When the Master let them go, the sun was already low, and the yards along the backs were half in shade. David ran all the way.

The shadow of the end house had come halfway across their own yard. Half the hedge was in it, and so was the black cloth, and Rebecca was sitting on the form beside the bag with her chin in her hands.

"It's out," she said. "It went at two, when the shade came on it. Before that it was nine. I only lifted the lid the once." She looked at him sideways. "The wee one had its feed till the half hour, like Ma said, and then I put the string back in the other."

The far end of the hedge still had the sun on it. They carried everything along together, cloth and cord and bag and machine, and hung the cloth there.

The light came back.

"In the shade it goes out," said David. "In the sun it comes back."

"I know," said Rebecca. "I've been watching it since dinner-time."

He lifted the lid. There was the little white picture, and the lightning, and:

*9%*

But the sun was going down behind the hedges across the backs, and in November the afternoons are short. The light came, and went, and came again, like somebody trying hard to keep his eyes open. Then it went, and didn't come back.

David sat down on the form beside her.

"It'll never run down now," he said. "Not ever. We'll put the cloth out every day, and it can write all day long, and feed while it's at it."

"If there's sun," said Rebecca.

"There's always sun."

"This is Ballyclare," said Rebecca.

"In," said Ma, from the back door. "The frost'll be down again by dark. In with the lot of it."

So the washing came in off the hedges, stiff and cold and smelling of wind, and the black cloth came in with it, folded shut on its glass and strapped back onto the bag. The cord stayed in the side of the writing machine. Nobody thought of taking it out now. The wee glass was put away in its pocket at the top, dark again, and the bag went back up on the sill of the room, out of the damp.

Ma stood a moment in the room door, looking at the bag on the sill and the man in the bed, as if she were counting them.

At six the mill horn went. A while after it they heard Da's step outside, and his hand on the latch.

Ma put one hand on David's shoulder, and the other on Rebecca's.

"Not a word of this," she said, "to your father."
