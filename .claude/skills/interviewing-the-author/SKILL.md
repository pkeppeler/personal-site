# Interviewing the author

Posts are written about things the author has built, experienced, or
learned. Before drafting, surface enough of that raw material — prior
sessions, notes, memory, opinions not yet articulated — through an
interview.

## How to run it

1. **Ask in rounds, not all at once.** Three to six questions per round,
   grouped by theme (motivation, decisions, surprises, process, future).
   Number each question and put your recommended answer underneath it.
   Wait for the author's replies before asking the next round — don't
   drip one question at a time, and don't dump the whole bank in one wall.
2. **Propose options, not abstractions.** Authors react to concrete
   alternatives faster than to abstract descriptions. Offer 4–6 title
   candidates. When tone is unsettled, offer 4–6 labelled tone samples of
   the same paragraph (e.g. "A. casual grumpy-engineer", "B. drier
   matter-of-fact") and let the author pick or remix.
3. **Confirm framing before drafting.** Settle the through-line, target
   audience, and target length — don't start writing until these hold.
   A draft written against an unsettled through-line gets thrown away
   rather than edited, so guessing here costs the whole draft, not a
   revision.
4. **Summarize back before drafting.** Restate the framing, tone, length,
   and any specific phrasings you plan to preserve verbatim from the
   author's own words. Draft only after the author confirms.

## Question bank

Not every post needs all ten, but the answers that land usually come
from this list.

1. What triggered this? A specific moment, frustration, or observation.
   The more concrete the better.
2. What's the obvious alternative, and why didn't you do that? The
   novelty of the post usually lives here.
3. What would have happened if you hadn't built / done this? Forces the
   author to articulate the real cost of inaction.
4. What surprised you, good or bad? Unexpected hurdles or unexpected
   wins.
5. What was easier than expected? Surfaces counterintuitive leverage for
   readers.
6. Where did you do this, specifically? Train, cafe, back of a taxi,
   kitchen table. Sensory detail makes a post feel lived-in.
7. Who's the target reader? Other engineers? Generalists? This changes
   how much to explain.
8. What's the takeaway you want readers to leave with? A heuristic, a
   pattern, a recipe, or just a reframe.
9. What's next? Does this unlock anything?
10. Any personal details or caveats that matter? People involved,
    inconsistencies to flag, alternatives that exist but weren't chosen.

## Authorship

A post drafted through this interview gets `authorship: ai-drafted` in
its frontmatter.
